Foundations

1 The objects behind the statement.

Write a complex number as \(s=\sigma+it\). Its real part \(\sigma\) measures horizontal position in the diagrams. The vertical line \(\sigma=\tfrac12\) is the critical line.

The first object

1.1 A function that remembers the primes.

For \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\), the Riemann zeta function has both a sum over positive integers and a product over primes:

\[\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s} =\prod_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}.\]

Unique factorization explains the link. Expanding each geometric factor selects a power of each prime; multiplying collects every positive integer exactly once. Absolute convergence in this region makes that rearrangement legitimate. The product also gives nonvanishing there.

Analytic continuation extends \(\zeta\) to a meromorphic function on the complex plane, with a simple pole at \(s=1\). The original series is not a formula for directly evaluating it throughout the critical strip. Zeros at \(-2,-4,-6,\ldots\) are called trivial zeros. The remaining zeros lie in the open strip \(0<\sigma<1\); RH says that their real part is always \(\tfrac12\).

\[\zeta(\rho)=0,\quad 0<\operatorname{Re}\rho<1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Re}\rho=\tfrac12.\]

The archive also uses a formulation that explicitly excludes the negative even integers and \(s=1\). These exceptions matter when comparing a familiar textbook statement with an exact formal declaration. Read the target statements.

Why does a line of zeros concern prime numbers?

In the absolutely convergent region, differentiating the Euler product gives

\[-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} =\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{n^s}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}s>1.\]

Here the von Mangoldt function is \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(p^k)=\log p\) for prime powers and is zero otherwise. Zeros of \(\zeta\) become poles of its logarithmic derivative. Through explicit formulas, their locations govern oscillations in prime-counting quantities. This explains why controlling zeros is arithmetic information; the detailed explicit-formula argument is outside this chapter.

The larger family

1.2 Twist the integers by a character.

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer \(q\) is a multiplicative character of the finite group \((\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times\), extended periodically to the integers and set to zero on integers not coprime to \(q\). On units its values are roots of unity. The principal character \(\chi_0\) is one on units and zero elsewhere.

\[L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} =\prod_p(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}s>1.\]

These functions continue meromorphically. Nonprincipal characters give entire functions; a principal character gives a simple pole at \(1\). Their zeros in the open critical strip form the family addressed here. DLMF §25.15 gives the classical definitions and identities.

A character is primitive when it is not induced from a character of a proper divisor of its modulus. Its conductor then equals \(q\). The clean functional equation used below applies to primitive characters. For an imprimitive character, one first relates its \(L\)-function to a primitive one, accounting for finitely many Euler factors.

The inverse character \(\chi^{-1}\) equals the complex conjugate \(\overline\chi\): a unit-modulus value has inverse equal to its conjugate, and both extensions are zero off the units. Inversion preserves the conductor, so the inverse of a primitive character is again primitive. This is why the argument can change characters without losing the functional equation.

A precise scope. “Dirichlet GRH” here means the critical-line assertion for Dirichlet \(L\)-functions. It does not mean a theorem about every automorphic or other \(L\)-function. The source exposes several formulations, including a primitive-character statement and a statement for all characters in the open strip.

The symmetry

1.3 Complete the function before reflecting it.

A primitive character has parity \(a\in\{0,1\}\), defined by \(\chi(-1)=(-1)^a\). In a conventional normalization, the completed function is

\[\Lambda(s,\chi)= \left(\frac q\pi\right)^{(s+a)/2} \Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+a}{2}\right)L(s,\chi).\]

The positive real base \(q/\pi\) fixes the power unambiguously through the real logarithm. Its complex exponential never vanishes. In the open strip, \(\operatorname{Re}((s+a)/2)>0\), so the gamma factor is finite and nonzero. Consequently \(L(s,\chi)\) and \(\Lambda(s,\chi)\) have the same zeros there. The classical fact used is that the gamma function has no zeros and only nonpositive-integer poles.

The functional equation has the symmetric form

\[\Lambda(s,\chi)= \varepsilon(\chi)\Lambda(1-s,\overline\chi), \qquad |\varepsilon(\chi)|=1.\]

The constant \(\varepsilon(\chi)\) is the root number. This is the completed version of the primitive Dirichlet functional equation. For the character modulo one, it is interpreted meromorphically; the poles at \(0\) and \(1\) do not meet the open strip.

The transformation is \(s\mapsto1-s\), so \(\sigma+it\) goes to \(1-\sigma-it\), and \(\chi\) changes to \(\overline\chi\). It exchanges the two sides of the critical line. For a nonreal character it relates two different \(L\)-functions; one must not silently keep the same character.

The precise conclusion

2.1 Nonvanishing to the right of one half.

Call the following proposition \(H\). For every positive modulus, every Dirichlet character \(\psi\), and every complex number \(w\),

\[\operatorname{Re}w>\tfrac12,\quad (\psi\ne\chi_0\ \text{or}\ w\ne1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L(w,\psi)\ne0.\]

The alternative in parentheses excludes the principal-character pole. This is the source proposition UpperTheory.EveryCharacter. The universal quantifier is essential: the left-side case will apply \(H\) to \(\chi^{-1}\), not just to the original character. Inspect its exact definition.

From arithmetic to analysis

2.3 Control a smoothed prime sum on long windows.

2.3.1 Separate the prime sum from its main term.

The quantity bounded by the arithmetic machinery is a corrected, smoothed von Mangoldt sum. Put \((t)_+=\max(t,0)\) and write \(\gamma_E\) for Euler’s constant. The source defines

\[A_\chi(y)=\sum_{1\le n\le\lfloor y\rfloor} \Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)\chi(n)(y/n-1)_+,\] \[G_\chi(y)=\begin{cases} A_\chi(y)-y\log y+(1+\gamma_E)y,&\chi=1,\\ A_\chi(y)+y\,L'(1,\chi)/L(1,\chi),&\chi\ne1. \end{cases}\]

The formal sum includes zero, whose von Mangoldt coefficient is zero; it can be read as a sum over positive integers. In the analytic families, a principal character is allowed only at modulus one. The correction therefore removes the zeta main term in that case. Definitions of ramp, Gchi and admissibility.

2.3.2 See what the ramp does to the counting function.

Define the character-weighted counting function \(\psi_\chi(t)=\sum_{1\le n\le t}\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)\chi(n)\). Each prime power enters this function abruptly. In the smoothed sum, its contribution starts at zero when \(y=n\), then grows linearly. The precise relationship, for \(y\ge1\), is

\[ A_\chi(y)=y\int_1^y\frac{\psi_\chi(t)}{t^2}\,dt. \]

To check it, interchange the finite sum and the integral. The term indexed by \(n\le y\) contributes \(y\int_n^y t^{-2}\,dt=y/n-1\). The endpoint \(n=y\) contributes zero. This averaging produces a continuous function while keeping the prime-power coefficients. It also creates the factor \(1/[s(s-1)]\) in the Mellin transform below. Smoothing changes how zeros are detected; it does not erase their contributions.

2.3.3 Combine the characters with positive weights.

A finite family \(F=(\chi_i,a_i)\) has strictly positive real weights and the real-valued error

\[G_F(y)=\sum_i a_i\operatorname{Re}G_{\chi_i}(y),\qquad a_i>0.\]

This is exactly the arithmetic level’s \(G_m\) after the comparison theorem identifies the families. Positivity concerns the weights; the error itself may have either sign. Family definition. Exact comparison.

2.3.4 Keep the exponent fixed while moving the window.

For each primitive character, the preceding construction supplies a level containing it and constants \(e_D,e_L,\varepsilon_1\), with \(\varepsilon_1>0\). For every \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_1\), it supplies a compatible family of windows. The constants \(e_D,e_L\) are chosen before \(\varepsilon\); the host/window family and the sufficiently-large threshold may depend on it. The analytic character family \(F\) attached to the fixed level stays fixed. On those windows, the arithmetic comparison gives

\[G_F(y)\le y^{\frac12+(e_D+e_L+7)\varepsilon}.\]

More precisely: beyond any requested lower bound, there is a whole interval \([Y,Y^\lambda]\) where this estimate holds. The final assembly uses \(\lambda=3/2\). These are arbitrarily late intervals, not an assertion about every sufficiently large real number. Quantifier order. Exact exponent. Meaning of a window. The choice \(\lambda=3/2\).

2.3.5 Choose an exponent to the left of a candidate zero.

The final choice of exponent is elementary and exact. Fix a candidate point with real part \(\sigma>1/2\), and set

\[m=\min(\sigma-\tfrac12,\tfrac12),\qquad \theta=\tfrac12+\tfrac m2,\qquad C_*=e_D+e_L+7,\] \[\varepsilon=\min\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_1}{2}, \frac{m}{2(|C_*|+1)}\right).\]

Then \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_1\), \(C_*\varepsilon\le m/2\), and \(1/2<\theta<\min(1,\sigma)\). Once \(y\ge1\), the arithmetic estimate implies \(G_F(y)\le y^\theta\) on arbitrarily late windows. The next theorem excludes zeros to the right of \(\theta\), hence excludes the candidate point. No substitution \(\varepsilon=0\) is required. The small-\(\varepsilon\) choice and its application.

The analytic mechanism · a step-by-step argument

2.4 Why one-sided windows exclude zeros.

A zero of an L-function leaves a pole in a transform of its prime-sum error. A pole, in turn, forces large high-order derivatives. The arithmetic window estimate puts an incompatible bound on a particular combination of those derivatives. We will build each connection, including the reason a bound on only some intervals is enough.

Throughout, \(F=(\chi_i,a_i)\) is the finite admissible family from the preceding section, \(a_i>0\), and \(G(y)=G_F(y)\) is its real-valued corrected prime sum. The arithmetic construction is an input to this analytic argument. Once it supplies the following estimate, the remaining reasoning concerns transforms, positive kernels and isolated poles.

2.4.1 State exactly what the window gives.

Fix \(0<\theta<1\), \(\delta>0\), and a real constant \(C\). The hypothesis is

\[ \forall Y_0\;\exists Y\ge Y_0\;\forall y\in[Y,Y^{1+\delta}], \qquad G(y)\le Cy^\theta. \]

There are three distinct pieces here. The windows occur arbitrarily far out. The bound holds at every point of each chosen interval. And its exponent and interval shape are fixed before the interval is chosen. The theorem concludes that no character in the family has an L-function zero with real part greater than \(\theta\), with the principal-character pole treated separately.

This is an upper bound on a signed function. A very negative value of \(G\) satisfies it too. Between the good windows we retain only the much weaker global estimate \(|G(y)|\le C_0y^2\) for \(y\ge1\), with a fixed \(C_0\ge0\). The proof must therefore control both the sign issue and the intervals where the sharper estimate is unavailable.

2.4.2 Turn prime-sum errors into an analytic function.

Set \(y=e^u\) and \(g(u)=G(e^u)\). The crude growth estimate becomes \(|g(u)|\le C_0e^{2u}\). Consequently the Laplace transform

\[ \Phi(s)=\int_0^\infty g(u)e^{-su}\,du =\int_1^\infty G(y)y^{-s-1}\,dy, \qquad \operatorname{Re}s>2, \]

converges absolutely and defines a holomorphic function there. The second expression is a Mellin transform. It makes the connection to L-functions visible: the smoothing factor in the prime sum has an elementary integral. For \(n\ge1\),

\[ \int_n^\infty (y/n-1)y^{-s-1}\,dy =\frac{n^{-s}}{s(s-1)} \qquad(\operatorname{Re}s>1). \]

Indeed, integrate \(y^{-s}/n-y^{-s-1}\) term by term. Multiplying by the von Mangoldt coefficient and the character, then summing in a half-plane of absolute convergence, brings in the logarithmic derivative

\[ \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)\chi(n)}{n^s} =-\frac{L'(s,\chi)}{L(s,\chi)}. \]

Here \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)=\log p\) when \(n\) is a positive power of a prime \(p\), and is zero otherwise. This identity follows by logarithmically differentiating the Euler product. It explains why information about primes can detect zeros: division by \(L\) exposes them as singularities.

For a nonprincipal character, the correction term in \(G_\chi\) gives the individual transform

\[ \Phi_\chi(s)= \frac{-L'(s,\chi)/L(s,\chi) +sL'(1,\chi)/L(1,\chi)}{s(s-1)}. \]

The numerator vanishes at \(s=1\), where the denominator suggests an extra singularity. Since \(L(1,\chi)\ne0\), this singularity is removable. For the principal character allowed in this family, the function is zeta; its separate main-term correction plays the corresponding role, using the holomorphic regularization of \((s-1)\zeta(s)\) at \(1\). Thus the relevant singularities in the positive half-plane come from zeros, rather than from the removed main terms.

2.4.2.1 Check the cancellation for zeta explicitly.

The zeta case explains the otherwise unusual coefficient \(1+\gamma_E\). Integrating its two correction terms gives

\[ \Phi_1(s)=\frac{-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)}{s(s-1)} -\frac1{(s-1)^2}+\frac{1+\gamma_E}{s-1}. \]

Put \(h=s-1\). The Laurent expansion of zeta starts with \(\zeta(1+h)=h^{-1}+\gamma_E+O(h)\). Consequently

\[ -\frac{\zeta'(1+h)}{\zeta(1+h)}=\frac1h-\gamma_E+O(h), \qquad \frac1{1+h}=1-h+O(h^2), \] \[ \frac{-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)}{s(s-1)} =\frac1{h^2}-\frac{1+\gamma_E}{h}+O(1). \]

The double-pole and simple-pole terms cancel exactly against the corrections in \(\Phi_1\). Here \(O(h)\) means a remainder bounded in magnitude by a constant times \(|h|\) near zero. The resulting meromorphic expression has a removable singularity at \(1\); the prime-sum correction was chosen to enforce this, not merely to make its average small.

2.4.2.2 Recombine the characters without losing analyticity.

Finally, \(G\) is a positive weighted sum of real parts. The identity \(\operatorname{Re}z=(z+\overline z)/2\), together with conjugation of Dirichlet characters, gives the paired transform

\[ \Phi(s)=\sum_i\frac{a_i}{2} \bigl(\Phi_{\chi_i}(s)+\Phi_{\chi_i^{-1}}(s)\bigr). \]

The transform identities continue \(\Phi\) meromorphically into \(\operatorname{Re}s>0\). Its defining integral is still used only where \(\operatorname{Re}s>2\). Analytic continuation supplies the larger domain; a sparse collection of good windows does not itself establish convergence of that integral throughout the larger half-plane.

2.4.3 Show that a zero produces a pole that cannot cancel.

Suppose \(L(s,\chi)\) has a zero of order \(m\ge1\) at \(\rho\), with \(0<\operatorname{Re}\rho<1\). Locally one can factor it as \(L(s,\chi)=(s-\rho)^m h(s)\), where \(h\) is holomorphic and \(h(\rho)\ne0\). Taking the logarithmic derivative yields

\[ \frac{L'(s,\chi)}{L(s,\chi)} =\frac{m}{s-\rho}+\frac{h'(s)}{h(s)}. \]

So even a multiple zero gives a simple pole of the logarithmic derivative. In the transform, its residue is \(-m/[\rho(\rho-1)]\). The other correction terms are holomorphic near this point. Adding the family’s contributions therefore gives

\[ \Phi(s)=\frac{c_\rho}{s-\rho}+H_\rho(s), \qquad c_\rho=-\frac{S_\rho}{\rho(\rho-1)}, \] \[ S_\rho=\sum_i\frac{a_i}{2} \bigl(m_{\chi_i}(\rho)+m_{\chi_i^{-1}}(\rho)\bigr)>0. \]

The multiplicity is zero when the corresponding function is nonzero. All multiplicities are nonnegative, at least one is positive, and every weight is positive. Every contribution shares the same complex factor \(-1/[\rho(\rho-1)]\), so they cannot cancel. Hence \(c_\rho\ne0\), and \(H_\rho\) is holomorphic near the pole.

A real zero \(\beta\in(0,1)\) has the stronger property \(c_\beta>0\): the denominator \(\beta(\beta-1)\) is negative. This sign will handle one of the two pole configurations later. Zeros away from the real axis need not have positive real residues.

The analytic setup also gives local finiteness: every compact subset strictly inside the positive half-plane contains only finitely many poles. This follows from isolated zeros of the finitely many nonzero analytic functions involved. It lets us isolate a pole on a bounded disc, even if the full set of zeros is infinite.

2.4.4 Measure a window after taking logarithms.

Choose a good window with \(Y>1\). The multiplicative interval becomes an interval whose length grows with its starting point:

\[ [Y,Y^{1+\delta}]\longmapsto[U,(1+\delta)U], \qquad U=\log Y. \]

On this interval, take the nonnegative majorant \(R(u)=|C|e^{\theta u}\). Then \(g(u)\le R(u)\), even if the original constant \(C\) was negative. After removing the expected exponential growth, its weighted mass is simply

\[ \int_U^{(1+\delta)U}R(u)e^{-\theta u}\,du =|C|\delta U\le e^{\eta U} \quad\text{for sufficiently large }U, \quad\eta>0. \]

Every positive exponential eventually dominates this linear function. Given any \(\eta>0\) and any requested starting point, we can therefore choose a good window late enough for this mass bound. The order matters: \(\eta\) is chosen first. We will later choose it small enough to contradict the growth forced by a pole.

2.4.5 Use high derivatives to look inside a late window.

At a fixed real center \(\tau>2\), define the normalized derivatives

\[ D_k(z)=\frac{(-1)^k\Phi^{(k)}(z)}{k!}. \]

Dividing by the factorial removes the routine factorial growth of derivatives, leaving the geometric growth that measures distance to a pole. Differentiating the convergent Laplace integral gives a factor \((-u)^k\); the sign in \(D_k\) cancels it. The global exponential bound justifies differentiation under the integral for each fixed \(k\). Thus, for every real \(t\),

\[ D_k(\tau+it)=\int_0^\infty g(u)w_k(u)e^{-itu}\,du, \qquad w_k(u)=\frac{u^k e^{-\tau u}}{k!}\ge0. \]

The kernel is a movable positive weight. For \(k>0\), logarithmic differentiation shows that its maximum occurs at \(u=k/\tau\). Raising the derivative order moves its main contribution farther out. Choosing \(k\) proportional to \(U\) therefore aims the integral at the good interval, while the factor \(e^{-itu}\), of absolute value one, changes only its oscillation.

2.4.6 Keep the term that makes an upper bound useful.

Taking the absolute value of the oscillatory integral gives \(|D_k(\tau+it)|\le\int_0^\infty |g|w_k\). At the real center, \(\operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau)=\int_0^\infty gw_k\). Adding these two relations is the crucial move:

\[ |D_k(\tau+it)|+\operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau) \le\int_0^\infty (|g(u)|+g(u))w_k(u)\,du. \]

For a real number \(g\), the expression \(|g|+g=2\max(g,0)\) discards its negative part. On a good window this is at most \(2R\). We have converted one-sided information into control of a nonnegative integrand.

To bound the window contribution, use \(v^k/k!\le e^v\) for \(v\ge0\), which follows because this is one term of the exponential series. Taking \(v=(\tau-\theta)u\) gives

\[ w_k(u)\le\frac{e^{-\theta u}}{(\tau-\theta)^k}. \]

Outside the window, use \(|g|+g\le2|g|\le2C_0e^{2u}\). Splitting the integral into its window and tails now yields

\[ \begin{aligned} |D_k(\tau+it)|+\operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau) &\le \frac{2e^{\eta U}}{(\tau-\theta)^k}+2C_0T_k,\\ T_k&=\int_{(0,\infty)\setminus[U,(1+\delta)U]} \frac{u^k e^{-(\tau-2)u}}{k!}\,du. \end{aligned} \]

Why the real-center term stays. The displayed estimate does not bound \(|D_k(\tau+it)|\) alone: \(\operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau)\) could be negative. The pole argument below either controls this term by analyticity or makes it positive using the residue of a real pole.

2.4.7 Make both uncontrolled tails small.

The tail kernel contains \(\tau-2\), because it must absorb the global bound \(e^{2u}\). Its maximum is at \(k/(\tau-2)\), slightly to the right of the original kernel’s maximum. Choose \(\tau\) sufficiently large depending only on \(\delta\), and then choose

\[ k=\left\lceil(1+\delta/2)\tau U\right\rceil. \]

The original kernel is aimed near the middle of the interval. A sufficiently large \(\tau\) keeps the tail kernel concentrated inside it as well. The width of the interval grows proportionally to \(U\); the concentration width grows only on the order of \(\sqrt U\) with \(\tau\) fixed. This explains why the window shape is effective. The following exponential estimate makes the intuition quantitative.

Put \(a=\tau-2\), \(V=(1+\delta)U\), and choose \(0<q<a\). On the right tail, \(e^{q(u-V)}\ge1\); on the left tail, \(e^{q(U-u)}\ge1\). Multiply by these factors and extend each nonnegative integral to the whole positive axis. The elementary gamma integral then gives

\[ T_k\le\frac{e^{-qV}}{(a-q)^{k+1}} +\frac{e^{qU}}{(a+q)^{k+1}}, \qquad \int_0^\infty\frac{u^k e^{-bu}}{k!}\,du=\frac1{b^{k+1}} \quad(b>0). \]

The integral identity follows by substituting \(v=bu\) and integrating by parts repeatedly: \(\int_0^\infty v^k e^{-v}\,dv=k!\). The boundary terms vanish because the exponential dominates each fixed power.

The first tail term penalizes points beyond the right endpoint; the second penalizes points before the left endpoint. Balancing these penalties with the choice of \(k\) gives a fixed constant \(T>0\) such that \(T_k\le T/\tau^k\) for every \(U\ge1\). The constant does not grow when a later window is selected.

Work through an explicit choice of constants

One choice used in the source is

\[ q=2+\frac6\delta,\qquad \tau=q(1+\delta)(q+2),\qquad T=\frac{e}{\tau-2-q}+\frac1{\tau-2+q}. \]

Both denominators are positive. Write \(A=(1+\delta/2)\tau\), so \(AU\le k\le AU+1\). Substituting these constants gives

\[ -q(1+\delta)U+k\frac{2+q}{\tau-2-q}\le1, \qquad qU+k\frac{2-q}{\tau-2+q}\le0. \]

For example, setting \(M=q(1+\delta)\) gives \(A=M(M-1)\) and \((2+q)/(\tau-2-q)=1/(M-1)\), so the first expression is at most \(1/(M-1)\le1\). For the second, \((1+\delta/2)(q-2)=q+1\), and the lower bound on \(k\) makes its negative term large enough.

For any positive \(b\), the inequality \(\log(\tau/b)\le(\tau-b)/b\) implies

\[ \tau^k\frac{e^x}{b^{k+1}} \le\frac1b\exp\!\left(x+k\frac{\tau-b}{b}\right). \]

Apply this first with \(b=\tau-2-q\), \(x=-qV\), and then with \(b=\tau-2+q\), \(x=qU\). The preceding bounds give exactly \(\tau^kT_k\le T\). Also \(k\ge U\), since \(\tau>2\) and \(U\ge1\).

Let \(E=\tau-\theta\). Since \(0<E<\tau\) and \(e^{\eta U}\ge1\), the tail bound can be absorbed into the window bound. With the fixed constant \(K=2+2C_0T\), we obtain

\[ |D_k(\tau+it)|+\operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau) \le\frac{K e^{\eta U}}{E^k} \qquad\text{for every }t\in\mathbb R. \]

Precisely: \(\tau\) and \(K\) are fixed first; for every \(\eta>0\) and every \(U_0\), there is a good \(U\ge U_0\) and an integer \(k\ge U\) for which this holds. We have not proved a bound for every derivative order. The arbitrarily large orders selected by the windows will be sufficient.

2.4.8 Isolate a pole while controlling the real-center term.

Assume for contradiction that a pole \(\rho_0\) lies to the right of \(\theta\). The transform is holomorphic for \(\operatorname{Re}s>2\), so every pole lies strictly to the left of our center line \(\operatorname{Re}s=\tau\). We need a pole close enough to some point on that line to force derivatives to grow faster than \(E^{-k}\).

There are two possible configurations. Their common ingredient is the behavior near one isolated pole. If a disc centered at \(z\) contains just that pole \(\rho\), write \(\Phi(s)=c/(s-\rho)+H(s)\) on the disc, filling the removable singularity of \(H\). For any smaller radius \(r\) within that disc, Cauchy’s derivative estimate gives

\[ D_k(z)=\frac{c}{(z-\rho)^{k+1}}+\varepsilon_k, \qquad |\varepsilon_k|\le\frac{M}{r^k}. \]

Here \(M\) bounds \(|H|\) on the circle of radius \(r\) and is independent of \(k\). Choose \(r\) larger than \(d=|z-\rho|\). The pole contribution then grows relative to the holomorphic error by a factor comparable to \((r/d)^k\).

2.4.8.1 A shifted center with a closer pole

There may be a radius \(D\le E\) such that the open disc about the real center \(\tau\) has no poles, but some pole has horizontal distance from the line \(\operatorname{Re}s=\tau\) less than \(D\). Move vertically to a center \(z=\tau+it\) near that pole’s height. Its distance is now less than \(D\). A small adjustment of the height makes the nearest pole unique; local finiteness allows us to choose a disc around \(z\) that contains this pole alone.

Choose \(d<r<D\), still inside that isolating disc. Cauchy’s estimate also applies to \(\Phi\) at the real center, because its disc of radius \(D\) is free of poles. Therefore

\[ |D_k(z)|\ge\frac{|c|}{d^{k+1}}-\frac{M_1}{r^k}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau)\ge-\frac{M_2}{r^k}. \]

The second bound limits how much the real-center term can cancel the first. The window estimate holds for every \(t\), so it applies at this chosen height.

2.4.8.2 A real pole with positive residue

The other configuration gives a real pole \(\beta>\theta\) that is uniquely nearest to \(\tau\) on a suitable disc. The already available nonvanishing for \(\operatorname{Re}s\ge1\) places this pole in \((\theta,1)\), so step 2.4.3 gives its positive residue \(c\). Set \(d=\tau-\beta\), and choose \(d<r\le E\) inside the isolating disc. At the real center,

\[ \operatorname{Re}D_k(\tau) \ge\frac{c}{d^{k+1}}-\frac{M}{r^k}. \]

Now this term itself supplies the lower bound. Set \(t=0\) in the window estimate and use \(|D_k(\tau)|\ge0\). This is the point at which positivity of a real pole’s residue is essential.

Why these two configurations cover every possible pole set

Look at the closed disc centered at \(\tau\) with radius \(E=\tau-\theta\). It lies entirely in \(\operatorname{Re}s\ge\theta>0\), so it contains finitely many poles. If it contains none, take \(D=E\); the pole \(\rho_0\) has horizontal distance \(\tau-\operatorname{Re}\rho_0<E\), giving the shifted-center configuration.

Otherwise let \(D\) be the smallest distance from \(\tau\) to a pole in that disc. The open disc of radius \(D\) is free of poles. If any pole has horizontal distance less than \(D\), use the same shifted-center configuration. If none does, a nearest pole \(\rho\) satisfies

\[ D=|\tau-\rho|\ge\tau-\operatorname{Re}\rho\ge D. \]

Equality forces \(\operatorname{Im}\rho=0\). Every nearest pole must therefore be the same real point \(\beta=\tau-D\). Moreover \(D<E\), because the originally assumed pole has horizontal distance less than \(E\). Thus \(\beta>\theta\), and finiteness lets us enlarge the disc a little while keeping \(\beta\) as its only pole. This gives the real-pole configuration.

For the shifted-center case, the small height adjustment has a concrete justification. Only finitely many poles can compete near the chosen center. The difference between two squared distances to \(\tau+it\) is affine in \(t\). Distinct poles to the left of the line cannot have identically equal distances along the line. Avoiding finitely many tie heights therefore makes the nearest one unique, while keeping its distance below \(D\).

This selection uses finite sets inside bounded regions. It never requires the entire set of zeros to have a rightmost member.

2.4.9 Compare growth rates and get the contradiction.

In either configuration, combine the pole lower bound with the window upper bound and absorb fixed constants. There are \(A>0\), \(M\ge0\), and radii \(0<d<r\le E\) such that every derivative order supplied by the window construction would have to satisfy

\[ \frac{A}{d^k}\le\frac{M}{r^k} +\frac{K e^{\eta U}}{E^k}. \]

Multiply by \(d^k\) and put \(q_*=d/r<1\). Since \(r\le E\), the ratio \(d/E\) is at most \(q_*\). Since \(k\ge U\), raising either ratio to the power \(k\) makes it no larger than raising \(q_*\) to the power \(U\). Hence

\[ A\le Mq_*^U+K e^{\eta U}q_*^U \le(M+K)\exp\!\left[-\bigl(\log(r/d)-\eta\bigr)U\right]. \]

Choose \(\eta=\tfrac12\log(r/d)>0\). The right side tends to zero as \(U\) grows, whereas \(A\) is a fixed positive number. The window hypothesis allows \(U\) beyond any requested threshold for this chosen \(\eta\), so the displayed inequality is impossible.

Only a sequence of suitable windows and derivative orders is needed: the contradiction holds once that sequence reaches a sufficiently large scale. The strict gap \(d<r\) supplies exponential growth that the arbitrarily small exponential allowance \(e^{\eta U}\) cannot absorb.

2.4.10 Translate the absence of poles back into nonvanishing.

We have proved that every pole of the transform in the positive half-plane has real part at most \(\theta\). A zero of any family member with \(\theta<\operatorname{Re}s<1\) would have produced a genuine pole by step 2.4.3, so such a zero cannot exist. The existing nonvanishing result on \(\operatorname{Re}s\ge1\) handles the rest of the region, with the principal-character pole at \(s=1\) excluded in the ordinary analytic formulation.

For the larger arithmetic argument, fix any candidate point with real part \(\sigma>1/2\). The preceding small-\(\varepsilon\) construction supplies the window estimate for an exponent \(\theta\) satisfying \(1/2<\theta<\min(1,\sigma)\). This Landau argument then excludes that candidate. Repeating the reasoning for each candidate establishes nonvanishing throughout the right half-plane.

What each hypothesis contributes. Positive character weights preserve the poles. The growing windows permit kernel localization. The crude global bound controls the tails. Isolated poles allow Cauchy estimates. Positive residues at real poles resolve the possible cancellation at the real center. Together these facts turn a one-sided arithmetic estimate into a statement about every zero.

The last extension

2.5 Restore the missing Euler factors.

The argument so far treats primitive characters. Suppose \(\chi\) modulo \(N\) is induced by \(\chi^*\) of conductor dividing \(N\). At the points under consideration, where \(\chi\ne1\) or \(s\ne1\), the change-of-level identity gives

\[L(s,\chi)=L(s,\chi^*) \prod_{p\mid N}\bigl(1-\chi^*(p)p^{-s}\bigr).\]

Factors for primes dividing the conductor equal one. For every remaining prime and \(\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1/2\),

\[|\chi^*(p)p^{-s}|\le p^{-\sigma}<1.\]

A factor could vanish only if its subtracted term equalled one, which has norm one. Thus every factor is nonzero, and the primitive result makes their finite product nonzero. The final statement retains the exception \(\chi\ne1\) or \(s\ne1\), excluding the principal-character pole. This is the complete extension to arbitrary Dirichlet characters used by plugAll. 2.6 From local arithmetic to prime-sum windows.

The Landau argument needs an upper bound for a real-valued prime sum on arbitrarily late intervals. The arithmetic window theorem supplies a different kind of information: it bounds the size of certain Galois-cohomology groups, simultaneously across primes and twists. Several comparison theorems carry that information into the prime-sum estimate. This section explains the objects being measured and follows the quantitative passage between the two statements.

The central distinction is between the arithmetic budget, which can be very large, and the normalized analytic error, whose exponent approaches one half. The bridge uses compatible automorphic and geometric constructions. A bound on cohomology lengths by itself does not imply nonvanishing.

2.6.1 Identify what can be trivial locally but nontrivial globally.

Fix a number field \(E\) and a rational prime \(\ell\). Let \(S_\ell\) be the finite places of \(E\) above \(\ell\). The group \(G_{E,S_\ell}\) retains the Galois information unramified outside those places: it is the absolute Galois group with the inertia outside \(S_\ell\) killed. This already incorporates one set of local restrictions.

For a positive integer \(k\), the coefficient module is

\[ M_{\ell,k}=(\mathbb Q_\ell/\mathbb Z_\ell)(-k), \qquad g\cdot m=\chi_{\mathrm{cyc}}(g)^{-k}m. \]

The quotient consists of \(\ell\)-power torsion classes. The twist changes how Galois automorphisms act on them: the cyclotomic character records their action on roots of unity, and here its inverse \(k\)-th power acts by scalar multiplication. The underlying additive group stays the same. Different values of \(k\) therefore probe different arithmetic actions on that group.

First cohomology can be understood through a compatibility equation. A continuous cocycle is a map \(f:G\to M\) satisfying

\[ f(gh)=f(g)+g\cdot f(h). \]

This is exactly the condition that the affine transformations \(g*x=g\cdot x+f(g)\) compose as a group action. A cocycle of the form \(f(g)=g\cdot m-m\) can be removed by changing the origin. Such cocycles are called coboundaries; \(H^1(G,M)\) is the space of cocycles modulo these changes of origin.

The arithmetic object in the window theorem is the localization kernel

\[ \operatorname{Sha}_\ell(E,k)=\ker\!\left[ H^1(G_{E,S_\ell},M_{\ell,k}) \longrightarrow \prod_{v\mid\ell}H^1(G_{E_v},M_{\ell,k}) \right]. \]

The local groups are represented in the source by decomposition groups at chosen places in an algebraic closure. A class in this kernel can be removed by a change of origin separately at each place above \(\ell\), but may still resist every single global change of origin. It records global arithmetic information invisible to these particular local tests.

Which local tests? The vanishing restrictions in this kernel are at the finite places above \(\ell\). Unramifiedness outside \(\ell\) is already built into \(G_{E,S_\ell}\). These are different conditions, and the definition does not impose triviality at every place. The symbol \(\operatorname{Sha}\) here refers to this specific cohomology kernel.

2.6.2 Measure the kernel by its number of simple layers.

The measured quantity is

\[ \lambda_{\ell,k}(E)= \operatorname{length}_{\mathbb Z_\ell}\operatorname{Sha}_\ell(E,k) \in\mathbb N\cup\{\infty\}. \]

Module length counts the simple layers in a composition series. Over \(\mathbb Z_\ell\), each such layer has \(\ell\) elements. For example,

\[ M=\mathbb Z_\ell/\ell^3\mathbb Z_\ell \oplus\mathbb Z_\ell/\ell^2\mathbb Z_\ell, \qquad \operatorname{length}(M)=5,\qquad |M|=\ell^5. \]

Thus, whenever the length is finite,

\[ \log|M|=\operatorname{length}_{\mathbb Z_\ell}(M)\log\ell. \]

This explains why the later bound weights length by \(\log\ell\): it puts groups at different primes on the same logarithmic size scale. Length is additive in short exact sequences of finite-length modules, matching multiplication of cardinalities. That makes it well suited to estimates obtained from cohomological exact sequences.

The coefficient module \(\mathbb Q_\ell/\mathbb Z_\ell\) is infinite. The theorem concerns the finiteness and size of the specified kernels inside cohomology. It allows nonzero kernels of positive finite length.

2.6.3 Put all the relevant groups under one budget.

For a twist cutoff \(t\) and a real budget \(B\), the condition called VVle E t B first requires finite lengths for every prime \(\ell\) and every \(1\le k<t\). It then requires, for every finite set \(P\) of primes,

\[ \sum_{\ell\in P}\log\ell \sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{\ell,k}(E)\le B. \]

Finiteness is a separate assertion, made before the lengths are used as ordinary nonnegative integers. The statement also quantifies over the algebraic closure and compatible local choices used to present these groups, so it does not depend on a convenient choice of presentation.

This is a shared budget. It bounds each individual length by \(B/\log\ell\), but also limits how much all of them can contribute together. For an illustrative finite collection, lengths \(3,1\) at prime \(2\) and length \(2\) at prime \(3\) contribute

\[ (3+1)\log2+2\log3 =\log(2^4\,3^2)=\log144. \]

The sum is the logarithm of the product of those group sizes. Every summand is nonnegative, so a large contribution at one prime cannot be canceled by another prime.

Bounding every finite partial sum also controls the full series. Its partial sums increase and stay below \(B\); their supremum is finite. We can therefore write

\[ \mathcal V(E,t)= \sum_{\ell\ \mathrm{prime}}\log\ell \sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{\ell,k}(E)\le B. \]

For the host's chosen field and local data, the later construction writes this full nonnegative sum as \(V\). The same bound therefore gives \(V\le B\). This is how many cohomology groups become one usable arithmetic error budget.

2.6.4 Choose fields adapted to the character being studied.

Fix the primitive character \(\psi\) whose L-function we want to study. The window theorem applies to a particular family of cyclotomic subfields. This is natural because a Dirichlet character already describes multiplicative symmetries of roots of unity.

Choose \(0<\varepsilon<1/52\), and round the desired size to a power of two by selecting a nonnegative integer \(e(x)\) for every \(x\ge1\), with

\[ x^{2+8\varepsilon}\le2^{e(x)} \le2x^{2+8\varepsilon}. \]

For example, taking the ceiling of \((2+8\varepsilon)\log_2x\) works. This introduces at most a factor of two while allowing a continuously varying scale \(x\) to determine discrete arithmetic data.

With two auxiliary primes \(q,d\), an eligible field embeds in a cyclotomic field whose conductor is divisible by the required moduli. Its fixing automorphisms, written as \(\zeta\mapsto\zeta^a\), are exactly those satisfying

\[ a\equiv\pm1\pmod{2^{e(x)+2}},\qquad \psi(a)=1,\qquad \left(\frac aq\right)=\left(\frac ad\right)=1. \]

The last two symbols are quadratic characters. Together, these conditions select the field by the symmetries we want it to retain. The construction is therefore organized around the target character and controlled auxiliary characters.

What conditions do the auxiliary primes satisfy?

At a scale \(Z\), the pair must satisfy

\[ q\equiv3\pmod8,\qquad d\equiv7\pmod{16}, \qquad q\mid d+1, \]

and every odd prime \(p\le Z^{\varepsilon/5}\) must divide \(d+1\). Their sizes are coordinated by

\[ \tfrac12Z^{\varepsilon/5}<\log q\le Z^{\varepsilon/5}, \] \[ \tfrac12Z^{\varepsilon/5}(\log Z)^2 <\log d\le Z^{\varepsilon/5}(\log Z)^2. \]

These congruence, divisibility and size conditions are the definition of an admissible pair in this argument. A separate prime-existence theorem supplies such pairs. The window statement itself says what happens for every pair meeting the conditions.

2.6.5 Read the arithmetic window in the right order.

The character \(\psi\), the small number \(\varepsilon\), and the entire rounding function \(e(\cdot)\) are chosen first. The theorem then supplies constants \(J\ge1\) and \(X_0\). They may depend on these initial choices.

  1. Take any \(X\ge X_0\), and any admissible prime pair \(q,d\) at scale \(X^{1/J}\).
  2. There is a lower endpoint \(Y\) such that \(X^{1/J}\le Y\) and \(Y^2\le X\).
  3. For every \(x\in[Y,Y^2]\), every eligible field at that scale satisfies the aggregate cohomology bound with the following cutoff and budget.
\[ t=\lfloor x^{401}\rfloor,\qquad B=\sqrt{\exp(x^\varepsilon)} =\exp\!\left(\tfrac12x^\varepsilon\right). \]

In particular, all positive twists below that cutoff have finite length, and their weighted sum across primes is bounded. The useful interval lies inside the larger range:

\[ [Y,Y^2]\subseteq[X^{1/J},X]. \]

The interval can depend on \(X,q,d\). Once it is chosen, the primes \(q,d\) stay fixed throughout it. The rounding value \(e(x)\), the cutoff, the budget, and the eligible number field can vary with \(x\). The theorem covers every eligible field at each point; it does not require one fixed field to serve the whole interval.

The lower endpoint escapes to infinity as \(X\) grows, because \(Y\ge X^{1/J}\) with fixed finite \(J\). This provides arbitrarily late usable intervals. Uniformity on the entire interval is what will ultimately matter to Landau.

Read the size in context. For fixed positive \(\varepsilon\), the budget \(\exp(x^\varepsilon/2)\) eventually exceeds every fixed power of \(x\). It is not a small absolute bound. Its usefulness comes from comparison with the still larger geometric scale already built into the construction, as step 2.6.8 shows.

2.6.6 Locate the substantial theorem behind the window.

The existence of these intervals is the deep arithmetic input. The source constructs it from the Galois-compatibility result explained in the next section, together with base change and local representation, automorphic, cohomological and CM inputs. The elementary facts about length above explain what the conclusion means; they do not establish that conclusion.

One layer of the construction is visible as a counting argument. For odd primes, it separates a finite exceptional set from the remaining primes; a separate part of the assembly handles the prime \(2\). The nonexceptional odd primes satisfy estimates polynomial in the relevant arithmetic level and twist cutoff. At exceptional primes, suitable layer bounds supply additional contributions. These nonnegative contributions are summed, and finitely many constants are absorbed into an aggregate estimate.

The supporting cohomological results include Poitou–Tate duality, local Tate duality, local finiteness, and generator bounds. They relate the localization kernel to other global and local cohomology groups so that the counting estimates can be applied. The duality statements carry their own hypotheses, including conditions at the prime \(2\); duality alone would not supply the required numerical bound.

The passage to a common window has a useful logical form. One theorem supplies an arithmetic window with prescribed properties beyond a threshold; another gives the cohomology estimate on any such window beyond its own threshold. Taking the maximum of the two thresholds lets the proof choose the interval once and apply both conclusions there. Two unrelated existence statements, each selecting its own interval, would not be enough.

Further construction and selection results arrange the fields and ranks needed by the automorphic argument. This chapter treats those deeper arithmetic results as inputs and explains their quantitative use below. The Galois representation section and theta and geometric sections describe the other branches that must be made compatible with them.

2.6.6.1 Pay for many coefficient primes with one integer.

A separate bound at each prime would not control their total: the number of contributing primes might grow. For the generic character components, the source instead bounds their lengths by valuations of a single nonzero integer. This is where the Galois representations become quantitative.

Fix a positive twist \(r\) and an auxiliary Frobenius prime \(p\). Let \(q\ge7\) be the corresponding residue-field size and put \(B=q^r\). The automorphic input supplies a finite list of trace labels \((t_s)_{s\in S}\), with \(|t_s|\le3\), \(Bt_s\) algebraically integral, and the list stable under algebraic conjugation. Labels distinguish representation classes even when two numerical traces coincide. For a conjugation-stable orbit \(\mathcal O\) of character values \(z_\psi\), each a root of unity, form

\[ \tau_\psi=B^{-1}z_\psi+1+Bz_\psi^{-1},\qquad N_p=\prod_{\psi\in\mathcal O}\prod_s B(t_s-\tau_\psi). \]

Each factor is an algebraic integer. Conjugation permutes all the factors, so \(N_p\) is an ordinary rational integer. It is nonzero because

\[ B(t_s-\tau_\psi)=Bt_s-z_\psi-B-B^2z_\psi^{-1}, \qquad B^2>4B+1\ge|Bt_s-z_\psi-B|. \]

The last term cannot be canceled by the others. The upper estimate \(|B(t_s-\tau_\psi)|\le2B^2\) also gives a height budget:

\[ \log|N_p|\le|\mathcal O|\,|S|\,(2r\log q+1). \]

The substantive deformation comparison bounds the sum of the relevant normalized character lengths by \(2v_\ell(N_p)\), for the qualifying generic primes \(\ell\ne p\). Here \(v_\ell(\ell)=1\); normalized length means length over the coefficient discrete valuation ring divided by its ramification index. The expanded deformation argument in §2.7.6–2.7.15 explains the intervening cocycles, cotangent module, rigidity results and trace comparison. They are what justify using the integer to bound cohomology.

Choose a second auxiliary prime \(p'\ne p\) to cover the omitted coefficient prime. For any finite set \(P\) of qualifying primes, unique factorization now yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\ell\in P}\log\ell\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal O}L_\ell(\psi) &\le2\sum_{\ell\in P}v_\ell(N_p)\log\ell +2\sum_{\ell\in P}v_\ell(N_{p'})\log\ell\\ &\le2\log|N_p|+2\log|N_{p'}|. \end{aligned} \]

This bound does not grow with \(|P|\). A polynomial bound for the number of automorphic trace labels, followed by summation over character orbits and twists, supplies the generic contribution. The comparison back to field cohomology also accounts for conductor and index factors. Exceptional primes and the prime \(2\) require additional arguments.

Source entry point: the sum of normalized lengths over coefficient primes.

2.6.6.2 Choose a window where exceptional twists are small.

The exceptional-prime argument produces at most \(R\) troublesome twist indices. A coarse bound can handle them if they are small, but becomes too expensive at large twists. The interval selection therefore aims at a precise implication: for each exceptional \(k\), throughout the chosen window,

\[ k\le x^{C_t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k\le x^\alpha, \qquad 0<\alpha\le C_t. \]

A troublesome twist is either below the cheaper cutoff or above the full cutoff. It need not disappear. To see how one interval can achieve this simultaneously, write \(u=\log x\), \(a_k=(\log k)/C_t\), and \(D=C_t/\alpha\). The forbidden condition becomes

\[ a_k\le u<Da_k. \]

For \(X>1\), start with \(U=\log X\) and try \([U/2,U]\). If some \(a_k\) lies in \((U/(2D),U]\), move the upper endpoint down to \(U/(2D)\); that obstruction then lies above the new search range. At most \(R\) such reductions are needed. Exponentiating a surviving interval \([y,2y]\) gives

\[ X^{1/J}\le Y,\qquad Y^2\le X, \qquad J=2^{R+2}(C_t/\alpha)^R. \]

The arithmetic tower estimates supply the uniform bound \(R\); the interval selection is the elementary final step. The exceptional indices can change with \(X\), while the bound on their number stays fixed. That is enough to choose \(J\) before \(X\) and obtain the required arbitrarily late power windows.

Source entry point: the logarithmic interval-selection lemma.

2.6.7 Fit the arithmetic window to the host construction.

The next stage constructs a host: one collection of fields, ranks and selected arithmetic classes on which all the comparison estimates can be stated together. The host carries both the cohomology budget and the character information needed for the prime sum. Its field can vary with \(x\); the analytic character family attached to the fixed target level stays fixed.

Some room is reserved when passing from the arithmetic window \([W,W^2]\) to the later window \([Z,Z^{3/2}]\). A fixed field-size factor \(c\ge1\) is absorbed by a rescaling

\[ \kappa=c^{1/(2+8\varepsilon)},\qquad Z=\kappa W,\qquad x=\kappa x'. \]

Because \(\kappa^{2+8\varepsilon}=c\), the original rounding estimate becomes

\[ x^{2+8\varepsilon}\le c\,2^{e(x')} \le2x^{2+8\varepsilon}. \]

The fixed factor is now included in the desired size range. For \(W\ge\kappa^3\),

\[ Z^{3/2}=(\kappa W)^{3/2}\le W^2. \]

Thus the shorter interval fits safely inside the available scale range. For \(x\) in the desired interval, \(x'=x/\kappa\) is at least \(W\) and at most \(W^2\), so the original arithmetic estimate can be used there.

The exponent \(401\) also leaves a concrete amount of room. The ranks required by the host are separately bounded by \(x^{400}\). Once \(x'\ge\kappa^{400}\),

\[ x^{400}=\kappa^{400}(x')^{400}\le(x')^{401}. \]

The original twist cutoff is therefore large enough after rescaling. Decreasing a cutoff only drops nonnegative terms from the cohomology sum. Also \(x'\le x\), so its budget \(\exp((x')^\varepsilon/2)\) is at most \(\exp(x^\varepsilon/2)\). These elementary comparisons explain the spare exponent and the change of window shape.

Separate field, prime-existence, rank and short-norm constructions supply the remaining host conditions. In particular, the same host retains the target characters, controls complementary characters and their logarithmic derivatives at \(1\), and gives the required proportions of selected arithmetic classes. Estimates on unrelated families would not combine in the way used below.

2.6.8 Absorb the cohomology term into a larger geometric bound.

At a host scale \(x\), write

\[ L=x^\varepsilon,\qquad b=e^{x^\varepsilon}, \qquad V=\mathcal V(E,t)\le\sqrt b. \]

The letter \(L\) in this step denotes a positive scale parameter, not a Dirichlet L-function. Although \(V\) may grow, it is small relative to \(b\): the ratio \(V/b\) is at most \(b^{-1/2}\), which tends to zero.

A separate geometric and cohomological comparison bounds a nonnegative weighted local contribution \(\eta_j\) at each relevant lift index \(j\):

\[ \eta_j\le d_j \left[C\left(fm\,bL^2+(\log x)^2N_c\right)+V\right]. \]

Here \(d_j\) is a dimension factor, \(f,m\ge1\) are positive size parameters, with \(m\) the chain length, \(N_c\ge0\) is another size budget, and \(C\) is a comparison constant. This inequality is substantive input from the compatible geometric construction. It describes where the cohomology contribution enters; it is not a formal consequence of the definition of length.

Now the absorption is elementary. Put \(B_0=fm\,bL\). For sufficiently large \(x\), the construction identifies \(d_j=D_0\) and has \(L,b\ge1\). A fixed positive power eventually dominates the squared logarithm, so \((\log x)^2\le L\). Consequently

\[ V\le\sqrt b\le b\le B_0,\qquad fm\,bL^2=LB_0,\qquad (\log x)^2N_c\le LN_c. \]

Substituting these bounds and replacing \(C\) by \(|C|\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_j &\le D_0\bigl[|C|L(B_0+N_c)+B_0\bigr]\\ &\le D_0(|C|+1)L(B_0+N_c). \end{aligned} \]

The last step uses \(B_0\le L(B_0+N_c)\). The cohomology term has now been absorbed into a uniform bound with the same shape as the other geometric estimates. No inequality of the form \(\exp(x^\varepsilon/2)\le x^A\) has been used.

The resulting local bound is combined with rank, divisibility, slope, irreducibility and global comparison estimates. Their role is to tie the geometric quantities to the character sums using the same parameters and selected classes. This compatibility is the substantial bridge between the arithmetic budget and the following analytic estimate.

2.6.9 Obtain the square-root-scale prime-sum estimate.

Once the compatible bounds are in place, the character-sum comparison yields an estimate on the usable windows and beyond a threshold. Write \(C_{\mathrm{lev}}:=4\,o\ge0\), where \(o\) is the fixed nonnegative integer parameter of the character level. This is an ordinary constant coefficient, not big-O or little-o notation. The estimate is

\[ G_F(x)\le x^{\frac12+(e_D+e_L+6)\varepsilon} +C_{\mathrm{lev}}\cdot x^{\frac12+\varepsilon}. \]

The dot makes explicit that the second term is a constant times a power of \(x\). The constants \(e_D,e_L\) record losses in the comparison, with \(e_D+e_L\ge0\). The exponent \(1/2\) enters through this comparison of character sums and the geometric size estimates. It is not obtained by merely taking a square root of the cohomology budget.

The final simplification costs one extra \(\varepsilon\). For \(x\ge1\), the second power is no larger than the first. For sufficiently large \(x\), we also have \(1+C_{\mathrm{lev}}\le x^\varepsilon\). Thus

\[ \begin{aligned} G_F(x) &\le(1+C_{\mathrm{lev}})\cdot x^{\frac12+(e_D+e_L+6)\varepsilon}\\ &\le x^{\frac12+(e_D+e_L+7)\varepsilon}. \end{aligned} \]

All eventual thresholds can be met by choosing a sufficiently late available window. The important output is an estimate on the entire interval, with \(e_D,e_L\) fixed before \(\varepsilon\) is chosen. The host family and its thresholds may depend on \(\varepsilon\); those loss exponents do not.

2.6.9.1 Recover the prime sum from local arithmetic.

The comparison uses a finite abelian group \(\Gamma=(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2\times H\) to record arithmetic classes; write \(n=|H|\). At a prime \(p\), let \(I_p\) be the inertia subgroup and \(F_p+I_p\) the Frobenius coset. For a character \(\xi\) define

\[ v_\xi(p^j)=\frac1{|I_p|}\sum_{h\in I_p}\xi(jF_p+h). \]

Character orthogonality converts the condition that a prime power occupies a specified arithmetic class into a sum over characters:

\[ \frac1{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\xi\in\widehat\Gamma} \overline{\xi(g)}\,v_\xi(p^j) =\frac1{|I_p|}\mathbf1_{g-jF_p\in I_p}. \]

The conjugate on \(\xi(g)\) is essential. Local divisibility terms involve positive ramps \((a/p^j-w)_+\), where \(w>0\) and \((t)_+=\max(t,0)\). The identity

\[ (a/p^j-w)_+=w\bigl((a/w)/p^j-1\bigr)_+ \]

turns them into the same smoothing used in the analytic error. Logarithms of local ideal sizes supply the factors \(\log p\), with inertia and residue degrees accounted for in the arithmetic-class formula. Subtracting the matching main terms leaves character errors. Averaging over translations removes unwanted phases and produces the positive weighted family \(G_F\).

More precisely, let \(N_T\) count the selected translates, \(m\) be the chain length, and \(b>0\) its weight scale. A second selected subset of \(H\) has \(N_2\) elements and positive proportion \(\delta=N_2/n\); the parameter bounds below give \(\delta\ge x^{-\varepsilon}>0\). Put \(Q=N_Tmb\). After controlling the changes of weights and arguments, the averaged comparison reads

\[ \frac{Q\delta}{2} \bigl(G_F(x)-\Sigma(x)-4x^{1/2+\varepsilon}\bigr) \le E_x+\frac{2Q}{x}. \]

Here \(E_x\) is the lattice-comparison error and \(\Sigma(x)\) is the sum of the absolute errors of the discarded auxiliary characters. The chosen host supplies \(\Sigma(x)\le C_{\mathrm{lev}}x^{1/2+\varepsilon}\). This concerns the auxiliary characters; the target family remains on the left-hand side to be bounded.

2.6.9.2 Balance the four normalized costs.

Work at \(x\ge3\) with \(\varepsilon>0\). The decisive quantity is \(E_x/Q\). The source bounds \(E_x\le x^{C\varepsilon}B_x\), where the error bracket is

\[ B_x=N_Ta_E(1+m^2/k) +m\left(2nmbx^\varepsilon +\frac{N_2kbx^\varepsilon}{n^2x^{-\varepsilon}}\right). \]

The parameter \(a_E\) is the degree allowance and \(k>0\) is a size parameter in the comparison, unrelated to the Tate twist index above. The chosen parameters satisfy

\[ \begin{gathered} 0\le a_E\le2xb,\qquad N_T\ge\tfrac12nx^{-\varepsilon/8}, \qquad x^{-\varepsilon}\le\delta\le1,\\ x^{1/2-\varepsilon}\le m\le x^{1/2+\varepsilon/2}, \qquad xm\le k\le n^2x^{1/2-\varepsilon}. \end{gathered} \]

Dividing each term by \(Q\) shows what those choices accomplish:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{a_E}{mb}&\le2x/m\le2x^{1/2+\varepsilon},\\ \frac{a_Em}{bk}&\le2xm/k\le2,\\ 2\frac n{N_T}mx^\varepsilon&\le4x^{1/2+13\varepsilon/8},\\ \frac{N_2}{N_T}\frac{k}{n^2}x^{2\varepsilon} &\le2x^{1/2+9\varepsilon/8}. \end{aligned} \]

The first cost falls as the chain gets longer; the third grows with its length. Ignoring the explicitly displayed small powers for intuition, they behave as \(x/m\) and \(m\), balanced at \(m\approx\sqrt x\). Adding all four bounds gives \(B_x/Q\le10x^{1/2+13\varepsilon/8}\). Dividing the previous comparison by \(Q\delta/2\), and using \(\delta^{-1}\le x^\varepsilon\), gives for \(x\ge3\) and \(C\ge0\)

\[ G_F(x)-\Sigma(x) \le26x^{1/2+(C+21/8)\varepsilon} \le x^{1/2+(C+3)\varepsilon}. \]

The last inequality holds once \(26\le x^{3\varepsilon/8}\). Substituting \(C=e_D+e_L+3\) and restoring \(\Sigma\) gives exactly the estimate at the start of step 2.6.9. Thus the square-root scale comes from balancing normalized comparison costs.

2.6.10 Hand Landau precisely its required hypothesis.

Fix a candidate zero with real part \(\sigma>1/2\), and choose

\[ \tfrac12<\theta<\min(1,\sigma). \]

The construction works for every sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Since \(e_D,e_L\) were fixed earlier, choose \(\varepsilon\) small enough that

\[ (e_D+e_L+7)\varepsilon\le\theta-\tfrac12. \]

For \(x\ge1\), the preceding estimate now implies

\[ G_F(x)\le x^\theta \qquad\text{throughout arbitrarily late }[Z,Z^{3/2}]. \]

This is exactly the Landau window hypothesis with \(C=1\) and \(\delta=1/2\). The step-by-step Landau argument excludes every family zero with real part greater than \(\theta\), and therefore excludes the candidate at real part \(\sigma\). The target character was retained in the fixed family, so the conclusion applies to it.

For each candidate point, one chooses a suitable positive \(\varepsilon\) and then an arbitrarily late window. There is no need for one common window to serve all candidate zeros, or for an estimate at the unavailable value \(\varepsilon=0\).

From automorphic forms to arithmetic

2.7 Build a representation that remembers the arithmetic.

The cohomology estimates need a connection between automorphic forms and field symmetries. The Galois-representation theorem supplies that connection, with precise local restrictions. We first separate what the representation is, what information it must retain, and how the source constructs it. Then §2.7.6–2.7.15 follows the quantitative passage through deformation rings, cotangent modules and trace congruences.

2.7.1 Turn field symmetries into linear algebra.

An automorphic representation \(\pi\) organizes symmetries of automorphic forms. A Galois representation assigns an invertible matrix to each automorphism of an algebraic closure, preserving composition:

\[ r:G_E=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline E/E)\longrightarrow \operatorname{GL}_a(\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell), \qquad r(gh)=r(g)r(h). \]

Here \(E\) is a CM field, a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real field, and \(\ell\) is a prime. The source starts with a regular algebraic cuspidal \(\pi\) of rank \(a\geq1\), satisfying its polarization condition. For each coefficient identification \(\iota:\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\cong\mathbb C\), the theorem supplies a continuous, semisimple \(r\). Semisimplicity means that the representation splits into irreducible pieces; continuity respects the Galois and \(\ell\)-adic topologies.

2.7.2 Compare information that does not depend on a basis.

A change of basis replaces a matrix \(A\) by \(BAB^{-1}\). Its entries change, but its characteristic polynomial and trace do not:

\[ P_A(T)=\det(TI-A),\qquad P_{BAB^{-1}}(T)=P_A(T),\qquad \operatorname{tr}(BAB^{-1})=\operatorname{tr}(A). \]

These invariants let arithmetic and automorphic constructions be compared without choosing the same basis. Frobenius is the local arithmetic operator whose eigenvalues enter familiar unramified comparisons. At ramified places, a Weil–Deligne parameter records both a Weil-group representation and a nilpotent monodromy operator \(N\). This section’s polynomial formula explains the language of the comparison; it does not assert unrestricted Frobenius compatibility. The actual local conclusions are stated below.

2.7.3 Retain the infinity type and its conjugation symmetry.

The theorem also supplies an integer \(\mathrm{wt}\) and integer multisets \(\mathrm{HT}(\tau)\), one for each embedding \(\tau:E\to\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\). If \(Z_{\iota\tau}\) denotes the automorphic infinitesimal-character multiset, the source fixes the normalization by

\[ \{h:h\in\mathrm{HT}(\tau)\} =\left\{\frac{a-1}{2}-z:z\in Z_{\iota\tau}\right\}. \]

This is a multiset equality, retaining multiplicities and viewing the integers in \(\mathbb C\). Complex conjugation \(c_E\) on the CM field pairs the weights:

\[ \mathrm{HT}(\tau\circ c_E) =\{\mathrm{wt}-h:h\in\mathrm{HT}(\tau)\}. \]

Thus a weight on one embedding determines its partner on the conjugate embedding. Above \(\ell\), the theorem identifies these multisets with the appropriate jumps under its comparison hypotheses. In the spherical local case it supplies crystalline and de Rham properties; the separate “nice” case supplies de Rham, potentially semistable, jump, and local-parameter conclusions under additional conditions. It does not declare every local component crystalline.

2.7.4 Follow the construction from geometry to the final representation.

The construction has two different tasks. First, geometric cohomology supplies representations for specially arranged automorphic forms. Then congruences and trace limits remove those extra arrangements. Theta lifting changes the rank, and descent removes auxiliary field conditions. Keeping these tasks separate explains why neither a geometric representation of the wrong dimension nor a collection of matching traces is already the final theorem.

2.7.4.1 Extract the automorphic information from geometric cohomology.

The geometric input contains a tower with Hecke correspondences, a chosen system of Hecke eigenvalues, and an algebraic character used to normalize the result. The cohomology of the tower carries a Galois action. Correspondences defined over the ground field commute with that action, so selecting their eigendata leaves a Galois-stable piece. In the familiar generalized-eigenspace notation, the mechanism is

\[ V_\theta=\bigcap_T\ker(T-\theta(T))^{m_T}, \qquad \rho(g)T=T\rho(g) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho(g)V_\theta\subseteq V_\theta. \]

This formula explains the selection mechanism; the source implements it through its geometric tower and matrix-realization conditions. Passing to the semisimplification retains traces and characteristic polynomials. The first output can have dimension \(d\) larger than the desired automorphic rank \(a\). Further extraction is therefore required.

At a suitable place away from \(\ell\), the geometric Hecke–Frobenius relation gives an annihilating polynomial. Schematically,

\[ Q_w(F_w)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{every eigenvalue of }F_w\text{ is a root of }Q_w. \]

An annihilating polynomial controls possible eigenvalues; it does not by itself give their multiplicities or identify the characteristic polynomial. The geometric package also records Hodge occurrences and crystalline comparisons at the specified coefficient-prime places. These additional data are used when extracting the rank-\(a\) seed.

2.7.4.2 Remove the extra character and arrange usable seeds.

The geometric eigendata include an algebraic Hecke character \(\kappa\). If the geometric polynomial has roots \(\alpha_j\kappa(w)^{-1}\), twisting by the corresponding Galois character restores the intended roots:

\[ \{\alpha_j\kappa(w)^{-1}\}_j \xrightarrow{\ \otimes\kappa_\ell\ } \{\alpha_j\}_j. \]

Weights must be corrected at the same time: tensoring by a character adds its Hodge–Tate weight to every weight of the representation. Correcting the eigenvalues while leaving the weights unchanged would give the wrong normalization.

The first seed theorem works in odd rank with auxiliary field conditions, two selected split places above \(\ell\), specified local automorphic type, separated weights, and genericity conditions at finitely many chosen places. Its output is a continuous semisimple rank-\(a\) representation. It has a dense-set Frobenius comparison and stronger local conclusions at the finite set being controlled. These hypotheses make the geometric extraction possible; the target automorphic representation need not already satisfy all of them.

The quantifier order matters. For every finite collection \(Y\) of places we want to inspect, the construction may choose a suitable seed. The eventual representation must work for all places, so the next steps coordinate these separate choices.

2.7.4.3 Approximate entire eigenvalue systems, including their relations.

Write \(\theta=(\theta_j)_{j\in J}\) for the target Hecke eigenvalues and \(\eta_i=(\eta_{i,j})_{j\in J}\) for systems carried by usable seeds \(\rho_i\). The congruence theorem supplies more than approximation of each coordinate separately. For every \(\epsilon>0\), it supplies a finite set \(I_\epsilon\) and coefficients \(c_i\) such that, simultaneously for every integral polynomial in finitely many coordinates,

\[ \left|P(\theta)-\sum_{i\in I_\epsilon}c_iP(\eta_i)\right|_\ell \le\epsilon, \qquad P\in\mathbb Z[X_j:j\in J]. \]

This is the source's polynomial approximation condition. The coefficients need not be positive, and the target need not be the limit of a single sequence of eigenforms. Taking \(P=X_j\) controls an individual eigenvalue. Taking a polynomial relation controls how different eigenvalues fit together. That second feature is essential for eventually recovering a representation.

The same approximation can include finitely many local power-sum coordinates above \(\ell\), while retaining fixed weights away from the two distinguished places. The congruence theorem also places the target eigenvalues in a finite extension \(L/\mathbb Q_\ell\). This supplies a complete coefficient field in which limits can be taken.

2.7.4.4 Extend the prescribed traces from dense arithmetic data.

Suitable Hecke operators give elements \(x_j\in G_E\). For each seed there is a set \(D_i\subseteq J\) on which

\[ \eta_{i,j}=\operatorname{tr}\rho_i(x_j) \quad(j\in D_i), \qquad \overline{\{x_j:j\in\bigcap_{i\in I_0}D_i\}}=G_E \quad\text{for every finite }I_0. \]

The finite-intersection clause lets one compare several seeds on a common dense set. Their traces are continuous. Consequently, a uniform estimate obtained on that set extends to every group element. The trace-limit theorem constructs \(t:G_E\to L\) with the same approximation bounds:

\[ \left|t(g)-\sum_{i\in I_\epsilon} c_i\operatorname{tr}\rho_i(g)\right|_\ell \le\epsilon \qquad(g\in G_E). \]

Where a prescribed coordinate is shared by all the relevant seeds, \(t(x_j)=\theta_j\). Completeness prevents the values from escaping the coefficient field; the uniform approximation by continuous traces gives continuity. Density alone would establish uniqueness of a continuous extension, but would not establish that an extension exists. The approximation estimates supply that missing existence argument.

2.7.4.5 Prove that the limiting function really is a trace.

A continuous function on a Galois group need not be the trace of a representation. Traces of \(a\)-dimensional representations satisfy polynomial identities. Two are

\[ t(1)=a,\qquad t(gh)=t(hg). \]

A further alternating identity encodes the dimension. One way to write the source's recursive form is

\[ \begin{aligned} A_0(t)&=1,\\ A_{m+1}(t;g_0,\ldots,g_m) &=t(g_0)A_m(t;g_1,\ldots,g_m)\\ &\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{m} A_m(t;g_1,\ldots,g_0g_j,\ldots,g_m),\\ A_{a+1}(t;g_0,\ldots,g_a)&=0. \end{aligned} \]

For example, when \(a=1\), the last relation is \(t(g)t(h)=t(gh)\): the trace is a character. In higher rank, it is the corresponding matrix trace identity.

Polynomial approximation preserves every such identity. If \(P\) vanishes on all seed traces, then

\[ |P(t)|_\ell =\left|P(t)-\sum_i c_iP(\operatorname{tr}\rho_i)\right|_\ell \le\epsilon \quad\text{for every }\epsilon>0, \qquad P(t)=0. \]

The function \(t\) is therefore a dimension-\(a\) pseudocharacter, meaning that it satisfies the trace identities. The characteristic-zero realization theorem then supplies a semisimple representation \(r\) with \(\operatorname{tr}r=t\). A separate continuity theorem for semisimple representations upgrades the continuity of this trace to continuity of \(r\). These are genuine steps: taking a linear combination of representation matrices would not preserve the multiplication law.

\[ \text{polynomially approximated traces} \Longrightarrow t\text{ is a pseudocharacter} \Longrightarrow r:G_E\to\mathrm{GL}_a(\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell), \quad r(gh)=r(g)r(h). \]
2.7.4.6 Carry local information through the limit.

Recovering a global representation does not automatically preserve crystalline behavior or monodromy. The source therefore chooses the approximating seeds separately for each finite set of places. At a place above \(\ell\), it keeps the relevant Hodge jumps fixed and approximates local Frobenius power sums jointly with the global traces. The closedness and comparison results then transfer the specified local conclusions.

Power sums explain why this carries polynomial information. If \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_a\) are eigenvalues, put \(p_j=\sum_i\alpha_i^j\). Newton's identities recover the characteristic-polynomial coefficients \(e_k\):

\[ e_0=1,\qquad ke_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e_{k-j}p_j, \qquad \det(T-A)=\sum_{k=0}^{a}(-1)^ke_kT^{a-k}. \]

Thus matching the appropriate power sums recovers the polynomial in characteristic zero. It still does not recover the monodromy operator by itself. That is why the proof retains its crystalline, fixed-weight, and local-parameter hypotheses rather than treating trace convergence as a universal local compatibility theorem.

Different choices of finite test set must yield the same global semisimple representation up to isomorphism: the prescribed traces agree on dense arithmetic data. This uniqueness allows a local conclusion proved using one convenient family of seeds to be attached to the common representation.

2.7.4.7 Reach other ranks by lifting, then removing known characters.

The geometric seed argument starts in odd rank. For an even target rank, the theta input supplies lifts to sufficiently large odd rank, with compatible characters and weights. The relevant weight inequality has the form

\[ b+m_W(v)\le2\lambda_{v,k} \qquad\text{for every archimedean }v\text{ and weight index }k, \]

where \(b\) is the target rank of the lift. Auxiliary characters are chosen along with \(b\) so that this inequality and the required restrictions of those characters hold. Merely choosing a large rank would not suffice.

The lift adds explicit character contributions to the Satake data. The source uses two lifts whose extra character families are disjoint. After matching characteristic polynomials on dense arithmetic data, the resulting representations satisfy an identity of the form

\[ P_{R_1(g)}(T)\prod_j(T-\chi_{2,j}(g)) =P_{R_2(g)}(T)\prod_i(T-\chi_{1,i}(g)), \qquad \chi_{1,i}\ne\chi_{2,j}. \]

Semisimplicity turns this identity into a statement about actual constituents. A character occurring on one side must occur on the other; disjointness prevents it from being absorbed by the other auxiliary family. It must therefore occur inside the corresponding \(R_i\), where it can be split off. Repeating this removes the known lines and leaves the desired lower-rank representation.

\[ R_i\simeq r\oplus\bigoplus_j\chi_{i,j}, \qquad P_{R_i(g)}(T)=P_{r(g)}(T)\prod_j(T-\chi_{i,j}(g)). \]

These equations describe the character-removal step after the prescribed twists. The proof supplies the stable summands before cancelling their polynomial factors. It also transfers continuity, semisimplicity, Hodge jumps and the permitted local comparisons to the remaining summand.

2.7.4.8 Remove the auxiliary field conditions by compatible descent.

Auxiliary CM extensions arrange splitting and local conditions needed earlier. Constructing a representation over an extension \(E'/E\) gives an action of the smaller group \(G_{E'}\); extending it to \(G_E\) requires compatibility. The source combines automorphic base change, comparisons on overlaps, and representation patching. In schematic form the compatibility being sought is

\[ r_{E'}|_{G_{E'E''}}\simeq r_{E''}|_{G_{E'E''}}, \qquad r_E|_{G_{E'}}\simeq r_{E'}. \]

Matching semisimple traces identifies the overlap representations. The descent theorem handles the further consistency needed to obtain the global action. Local properties are then descended by separate results for unramifiedness, de Rham and crystalline comparisons, and local Frobenius data. They are not all consequences of the abstract existence of \(r_E\).

2.7.4.9 State what this chain has established, and where its inputs enter.

The resulting assembly has the following shape:

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{geometric eigenspaces and local comparisons} \Longrightarrow\text{odd-rank seeds},\\ \text{seeds + congruences} \Longrightarrow\text{continuous semisimple trace-limit representations},\\ \text{theta lifts + character removal + descent} \Longrightarrow\text{the required ranks and fields},\\ \text{local comparison theorems} \Longrightarrow\text{the precise conclusions in section 2.7.5}. \end{gathered} \]

At this assembly entry point, the geometric realization package and the theta-lift existence statement remain explicit hypotheses, named GeoOne and TLiftK in the source. Writing a proposition or packaging its conclusions is not a proof of it. The geometric package is itself assembled from the geometric tower, Hodge, correspondence and realization results; the theta package carries its own character and weight conditions. The steps above explain how those inputs are used and why the intermediate constructions work. They do not infer that the final short assembly theorem proves all of arithmetic geometry or theta lifting on its own.

2.7.5 Keep the local conclusions at their stated strength.

At a place \(v\nmid\ell\), write \(X_v\) for the Weil–Deligne data from \(r\), and \(Y_v\) for the parameter of the algebraically normalized local automorphic representation. The comparison always gives

\[\dim X_v=\dim Y_v.\]

Under the source’s \(\mathrm{Tor}\) condition and \(N_{Y_v}=0\), it gives \(X_v=Y_v\). A different clause assumes the source’s tame and squarefree conditions, zero monodromy on \(Y_v\), a continuous field identification \(E_v\cong\mathbb Q_p\), and

\[ a=1\quad\text{or}\quad16a^2<p. \]

That clause concludes

\[ N_{X_v}=0,\qquad P_{X_v(g)}(T)=P_{Y_v(g)}(T) \quad\text{for every Weil element }g\text{ with }\deg g=0. \]

The degree-zero restriction matters: it is not a blanket equality for arbitrary Frobenius powers. These are the local comparisons available for subsequent arithmetic arguments.

A Galois representation does not, by its existence alone, bound a cohomology group. The source uses a particular character module, embeds its classes into a three-dimensional deformation problem, and then controls that problem using automorphy and commutative algebra. This expansion follows the generic odd-prime branch of that argument. The exceptional-prime estimates and the later selection of arithmetic windows remain separate steps.

Let \(\ell\) be the coefficient prime, let \(\mathcal O\) be the ring of integers of a finite extension \(E_\ell/\mathbb Q_\ell\), and write \(\varpi\) for a uniformizer. Fix a positive twist \(r\) and a finite-order character \(\psi\) of level \(N\), viewed as a Galois character with values in \(\mathcal O^\times\). Put

\[ \Psi=\chi_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{\,r}\psi,\qquad D=E_\ell/\mathcal O,\qquad g\cdot t=\Psi(g)^{-1}t\quad\text{on }D(\Psi^{-1}). \]

The relevant group, denoted here by \(\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}\), consists of classes that are trivial on decomposition groups at primes dividing \(\ell N\), and trivial on inertia at every finite prime. With \(G_q\) and \(I_q\) denoting those local subgroups, its defining restriction map is

\[ \mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi} =\ker\!\left[ H^1(G_{\mathbb Q},D(\Psi^{-1})) \longrightarrow \prod_{q\mid\ell N}H^1(G_q,D(\Psi^{-1})) \times\prod_q H^1(I_q,D(\Psi^{-1})) \right]. \]

Restriction to inertia being zero means unramified as a cohomology class; vanishing on a decomposition group is stronger. These are character-cohomology groups with specified local conditions. The earlier field-cohomology problem reaches them through character decomposition and comparison results; it is not being identified with the full adjoint cohomology of an arbitrary automorphic representation.

Which hypotheses delimit this branch?

The source fixes an auxiliary imaginary quadratic field \(K\), takes \(N\) even and \(r\ge1\), and requires \(\ell\ne2\), \(\ell\nmid\operatorname{ord}(\psi)\), \(\psi(-1)=(-1)^r\), \(\ell\nmid|\operatorname{disc}K|h_K\), and \(\gcd(|\operatorname{disc}K|,N)=1\). Its good-data condition additionally imposes a square-one condition on a derived character, a residual independence condition involving \(K\), and \(\ell-1\nmid2r\). Here \(h_K\) is the class number. These restrictions enable the markings, local comparisons and automorphy statements used below. The displayed bound is asserted only when that full condition holds; the separate exceptional branches handle other cases.

2.7.7 Choose a faithful representative of each torsion class.

Write \(\alpha=\Psi^{-1}\). A continuous cocycle \(z:G_{\mathbb Q}\to D\) satisfies

\[ z(gh)=z(g)+\alpha(g)z(h). \]

Two cocycles represent the same class when their difference is \((\alpha(g)-1)t\) for a fixed \(t\in D\). Thus simply counting cocycles would count changes of representative as new arithmetic information. A marking chooses \(g_1\) with \(\alpha(g_1)-1\) a unit and replaces \(z\) by

\[ \widetilde z(g)=z(g) -(\alpha(g)-1)(\alpha(g_1)-1)^{-1}z(g_1), \qquad \widetilde z(g_1)=0. \]

This normalization is independent of the representative: adding a coboundary changes both terms by the same amount. A normalized coboundary is zero, because evaluating it at \(g_1\) forces its defining \(t\) to vanish. Consequently distinct classes give distinct normalized cocycles.

Now take a class killed by a nonzero \(b\in\mathcal O\). Normalization commutes with multiplication by \(b\), so \(b\widetilde z=0\). Its values therefore lie in a finite coefficient module:

\[ \mathcal O/b\mathcal O\simeq D[b]=b^{-1}\mathcal O/\mathcal O, \qquad t\bmod b\longmapsto t/b\bmod\mathcal O. \]

On \(G_K\), the split reference representation is \(\rho_0=\operatorname{diag}(\Psi,1,\Psi^{-1})\). Embed a scalar value into a matrix by

\[ M(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\t&0&0\\0&-t&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_0(g)M(t)\rho_0(g)^{-1}=M(\Psi(g)^{-1}t). \]

The two off-diagonal positions transform by exactly the character acting on our coefficients. The minus sign makes this line compatible with the conjugate-self-dual extension of the construction from \(G_K\) to \(G_{\mathbb Q}\). The source consequently injects \(\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}[b]\) into a specified collection of normalized matrix cocycles. Their local vanishing and local coboundary conditions are retained. This explicit injection is the reason this particular cohomology group can enter the deformation problem.

2.7.8 Read the cocycle equation as a first-order representation law.

Introduce a formal infinitesimal \(\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon^2=0\). For matrix coefficients \(c(g)\), consider

\[ \rho_\epsilon(g)=(1+\epsilon c(g))\rho_0(g). \]

On \(G_K\), multiplying two such matrices gives

\[ \rho_\epsilon(g)\rho_\epsilon(h) =\bigl[1+\epsilon\{c(g)+\rho_0(g)c(h)\rho_0(g)^{-1}\}\bigr]\rho_0(gh). \]

The representation law holds precisely when \(c(gh)=c(g)+\operatorname{Ad}(\rho_0(g))c(h)\): the matrix cocycle equation. Conjugating by \(1+\epsilon X\) replaces \(c(g)\) by \(c(g)+X-\operatorname{Ad}(\rho_0(g))X\), a coboundary change. This explains the familiar link between first cohomology and infinitesimal deformations.

For torsion coefficients the source uses the square-zero algebra \(\mathcal O\oplus\epsilon(\mathcal O/b\mathcal O)\), whose multiplication is

\[ (a,\epsilon u)(a',\epsilon u') =(aa',\epsilon(au'+a'u)). \]

That retains the coefficient ring and the depth of the torsion. It also retains the conjugate-self-dual structure and the selected local conditions. The unrestricted slogan “the tangent space is \(H^1\)” would lose those restrictions and would not by itself give the bound we need.

2.7.9 Encode all admissible deformations in one ring.

A deformation ring \(R\) organizes lifts of the fixed residual representation into complete local coefficient algebras. After the source's basis normalizations and local conditions are fixed, its universal property has the form

\[ \{\text{admissible normalized lifts to }B\} \simeq\operatorname{Hom}^{\mathrm{cont}}_{\mathcal O,\mathrm{loc}}(R,B), \qquad \rho_B=f_*\rho_R. \]

The matrix entries of \(\rho_R\) serve as coordinates. Relations express the group law, the conjugate-self-dual symmetry, ramification restrictions and the chosen local deformation condition above \(\ell\). The marking removes specified changes of basis. Thus a cohomology class now gives a map from one fixed ring to a square-zero algebra, instead of a separately constructed representation.

The split representation determines a base map \(x_0:R\to\mathcal O\). A map reducing to \(x_0\) has the form \(f(a)=x_0(a)+\epsilon\delta(a)\). Multiplicativity is equivalent to the derivation rule

\[ \delta(aa')=x_0(a)\delta(a')+x_0(a')\delta(a), \qquad \delta|_{\mathcal O}=0. \]

This turns the deformation question into linear algebra on a module of differentials at the base point. No identification of \(R\) with a Hecke algebra is asserted in this passage; the source uses a more specific graded model and comparison theorem.

2.7.10 Identify the exact cotangent quotient being measured.

First recall the elementary construction. If \(J=\ker x_0\), a derivation vanishes on \(J^2\), since both factors have zero base value. Hence

\[ \operatorname{Der}_{\mathcal O,x_0}(R,Q) \simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(J/J^2,Q). \]

The quotient \(J/J^2\) is the cotangent module: it keeps linear changes and discards their products. The source refines this construction. It presents the constrained ring through a graded model algebra \(A\), a base map \(x:A\to\mathcal O\), and homogeneous equations. Diagonal conjugation gives weights \(-2,-1,0,1,2\) on matrix entries; the cocycles from §2.7.7 occupy weight \(-1\).

Set \(J_A=\ker x\). The source first saturates the square with respect to nonzero coefficients:

\[ J_A^{2,\mathrm{sat}} =\{a\in A:\exists d\in\mathcal O\setminus\{0\},\ da\in J_A^2\}, \qquad \Lambda_{-1}=\operatorname{im}\bigl(A_{-1}\cap J_A\longrightarrow A/J_A^{2,\mathrm{sat}}\bigr). \]

This forms a lattice of linear directions, with coefficient torsion removed. Let \(F_1,\ldots,F_m\) be the weight \(-1\) equations in the model, and let \(\overline F_i\) be their linear images in \(\Lambda_{-1}\). The module that enters the estimate is

\[ C_{-1}=\Lambda_{-1}/\langle\overline F_1,\ldots,\overline F_m\rangle_{\mathcal O}. \]

Its torsion measures how far the integral equations are from spanning the full lattice of relevant directions. Saturating the square did not discard this information: the torsion here comes from the index of the equation lattice. It is this selected quotient, rather than the whole ordinary cotangent module, whose length controls the character cohomology.

2.7.11 Measure the depth of every torsion layer.

A tangent-space dimension records how many directions remain modulo \(\varpi\). It does not record how long they survive. For example, \(\mathcal O/\varpi\mathcal O\) and \(\mathcal O/\varpi^{100}\mathcal O\) both have a one-dimensional quotient modulo \(\varpi\), but their lengths are \(1\) and \(100\).

Under the model's spanning hypotheses, elementary divisors put its equation lattice in diagonal form. After changing generators, with \(d\) the rank of \(\Lambda_{-1}\), its nonzero linear generators have the following form; any redundant linear generators can be taken to be zero:

\[ \overline F_i=\varpi^{a_i}y_i,\qquad C_{-1}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal O/\varpi^{a_i}\mathcal O, \qquad \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_i. \]

To see why this quotient controls cohomology, take a normalized cocycle with coefficients in \(\mathcal O/b\mathcal O\). The universal property produces a derivation on \(R\); composing with the model map \(A\to R\) produces a derivation \(\delta:A\to\mathcal O/b\mathcal O\) that kills the defining equations. The source's local lifting result supplies an integral, weight \(-1\) derivation \(\widetilde\delta:A\to\mathcal O\) reducing to \(\delta\).

This lift explains why the saturation causes no loss. If \(da\in J_A^2\) for \(d\ne0\), the derivation rule gives

\[ 0=\widetilde\delta(da)=d\,\widetilde\delta(a) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\widetilde\delta(a)=0. \]

The implication uses that the target \(\mathcal O\) is torsion-free. It would not be justified directly in \(\mathcal O/b\mathcal O\). Reduction now shows that \(\delta\) kills the saturated square; it already kills the equations \(F_i\). It therefore descends to a linear functional on \(C_{-1}\). The normalization and weight condition preserve injectivity of the original encoding, giving

\[ \mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}[b]\hookrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(C_{-1},\mathcal O/b\mathcal O). \]

For \(b=\varpi^n\), the elementary-divisor description makes the size comparison explicit:

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(C_{-1},\mathcal O/\varpi^n\mathcal O) =\sum_i\min(a_i,n)\le\sum_i a_i =\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}. \]

Since finite \(\mathcal O\)-modules have cardinality \(|\mathcal O/\varpi|^{\operatorname{length}}\), an injection gives the corresponding length inequality, even without asserting that our chosen encoding is \(\mathcal O\)-linear. For every nonzero \(b\in\mathcal O\), this yields

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}[b] \le\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}. \]

The local lifting property is essential to the comparison; the universal property of \(R\) alone would not imply this inequality. Every continuous cocycle from the compact profinite group \(G_{\mathbb Q}\) into the discrete module \(D\) has finite image. A common nonzero denominator kills that image, so every class is torsion. The subgroups killed by \(\varpi^n\) therefore increase to the whole group, and a bound independent of \(n\) gives

\[ \mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}=\bigcup_{n\ge1}\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}[\varpi^n], \qquad \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi} \le\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}. \]

This also establishes finiteness once the cotangent length is finite. Write \(e_{\mathcal O}=v_\varpi(\ell)\) for the ramification index and normalize by

\[ L_\ell(\psi)=\frac{\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\mathcal S_{\ell,r,\psi}}{e_{\mathcal O}} \le\frac{\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}}{e_{\mathcal O}}. \]

This is the normalization used with \(v_\ell(\ell)=1\). In particular, a factor \(\varpi^{a_i}\) contributes \(a_i/e_{\mathcal O}\) units of \(\ell\)-adic depth.

2.7.12.1 Fix a point and ask what can move.

Let \(\kappa=\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\), and choose a local characteristic-zero specialization \(f:R\to\kappa\) of the constrained deformation ring. The point retains the ramification, local type, polarization and marked normalization imposed on the universal representation. Its full representation \(\mathcal R_f\) includes a quadratic sign character \(\eta\); on \(G_K=\ker\eta\), for the imaginary quadratic field \(K\) in this construction, it is an ordinary three-dimensional representation \(\rho_f\).

The source distinguishes two sorts of relevant, two-sided points. In Type I, \(\rho_f\) is irreducible. In Type II, it has the prescribed outer rank-two block and middle line, with the outer block irreducible. Automorphy lifting supplies rank-three automorphy in the first case and modularity of a rank-two extension in the second.

Finiteness alone would not give the needed rigidity. A space can have one geometric point and still carry infinitesimal motion: \(\kappa[z]/(z^2)\) has just the point \(z=0\), but the map \(z\mapsto\epsilon\) into \(\kappa[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\) is nonconstant. Selmer vanishing is used to control these invisible first-order directions, not merely to count points.

2.7.12.2 Turn a first-order motion into an adjoint cocycle.

A tangent vector at \(f\) is an \(\mathcal O\)-linear derivation \(\delta:R\to\kappa\). Equivalently, it is a map into the dual numbers:

\[ F(b)=f(b)+\epsilon\delta(b),\qquad \epsilon^2=0, \qquad \delta(bb')=f(b)\delta(b')+f(b')\delta(b). \]

The symbol \(\epsilon\) keeps the first-order change and discards products of changes. Applying \(F\) to the matrix coefficients produces, on \(G_K\),

\[ \rho_\epsilon(g)=\rho_f(g)+\epsilon\dot\rho(g) =(1+\epsilon\gamma(g))\rho_f(g), \qquad \gamma(g)=\dot\rho(g)\rho_f(g)^{-1}. \]

Now expand the representation law and compare the coefficient of \(\epsilon\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_\epsilon(g)\rho_\epsilon(h) &=(1+\epsilon\gamma(g))\rho_f(g) (1+\epsilon\gamma(h))\rho_f(h),\\ \gamma(gh)&=\gamma(g)+ \rho_f(g)\gamma(h)\rho_f(g)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

This is the cocycle equation for the adjoint action. The global representation is polarized, so its action on all of \(G_{\mathbb Q}\) must retain the sign. With \(R_f(g)\) the matrix part of \(\mathcal R_f(g)\) and \(J\) the antidiagonal matrix, the coefficient representation \(V_f\) is the space of all \(3\times3\) matrices with action

\[ g\cdot X= \begin{cases} R_f(g)X R_f(g)^{-1},&\eta(g)=1,\\ R_f(g)(-JX^{\mathsf T}J)R_f(g)^{-1},&\eta(g)=-1. \end{cases} \qquad \gamma(gh)=\gamma(g)+g\cdot\gamma(h). \]

The negative transpose differentiates the duality condition in the polarization. In particular, this is not silently a trace-zero adjoint or ordinary conjugation on every Galois element. The source proves that the tangent cocycle is continuous as well as satisfying the algebraic identity.

2.7.12.3 Identify the motions that merely change coordinates.

Take a matrix \(X\), and change the basis by \(1+\epsilon X\), whose inverse is \(1-\epsilon X\). On \(G_K\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+\epsilon X)\rho_f(g)(1-\epsilon X) &=\rho_f(g)+\epsilon\bigl(X\rho_f(g)-\rho_f(g)X\bigr)\\ &=\bigl(1+\epsilon(X-g\cdot X)\bigr)\rho_f(g). \end{aligned} \]

Thus the cocycle \(\gamma_X(g)=X-g\cdot X\) represents an infinitesimal change of basis. The polarized action gives the same interpretation on the full group. The opposite sign convention \(g\cdot Y-Y\) describes the same coboundaries by taking \(Y=-X\).

\[ H^1(G_{\mathbb Q},V_f)=Z^1(G_{\mathbb Q},V_f)/B^1(G_{\mathbb Q},V_f). \]

This quotient distinguishes a genuine deformation from a coordinate change. Vanishing of the relevant class means that one single matrix \(X\) removes the motion for every Galois element. Finding different matrices locally at different primes is a weaker statement; the Selmer group measures the possible obstruction to making them compatible globally.

2.7.12.4 Restrict to motions allowed by the arithmetic.

The deformation problem does not allow arbitrary classes in \(H^1\). Away from \(\ell\), it requires the class to be unramified; at \(\ell\), it requires the crystalline finite condition. In customary cohomological notation, these local spaces are

\[ \begin{aligned} H^1_{\mathrm{unr}}(\mathbb Q_p,V_f) &=\ker\!\left(H^1(G_{\mathbb Q_p},V_f) \longrightarrow H^1(I_p,V_f)\right) &&(p\ne\ell),\\ H^1_f(\mathbb Q_\ell,V_f) &=\ker\!\left(H^1(G_{\mathbb Q_\ell},V_f) \longrightarrow H^1(G_{\mathbb Q_\ell}, V_f\otimes_{\mathbb Q_\ell}B_{\mathrm{cris}})\right). \end{aligned} \]

Here \(I_p\) is inertia and \(B_{\mathrm{cris}}\) is the crystalline period ring. The latter condition says that a local extension becomes split after adding the crystalline periods. Combining the local tests gives the global Bloch–Kato Selmer space:

\[ H^1_{\mathrm{BK}}(\mathbb Q,V_f)= \left\{[\gamma]\in H^1(G_{\mathbb Q},V_f): \begin{array}{ll} \operatorname{res}_p[\gamma]\in H^1_{\mathrm{unr}}(\mathbb Q_p,V_f) &\text{for }p\ne\ell,\\ \operatorname{res}_\ell[\gamma]\in H^1_f(\mathbb Q_\ell,V_f) \end{array}\right\}. \]

The source expresses this directly in terms of continuous cocycles and local coboundaries. Its polarized action and marked normalization also retain the complex-conjugation data. The subscript \(f\) in the usual local notation \(H^1_f\) means the finite local condition; it is not the name of our specialization.

The two assertions needed are now separate and precise:

\[ F\text{ an allowed tangent lift} \Longrightarrow[\gamma_F]\in H^1_{\mathrm{BK}}(\mathbb Q,V_f), \qquad H^1_{\mathrm{BK}}(\mathbb Q,V_f)=0. \]

The first comes from the construction of the constrained ring. The second comes from the vanishing inputs discussed below. Together they imply \(\gamma_F(g)=X-g\cdot X\).

2.7.12.5 Check why a permitted tangent satisfies the local tests.

Away from \(\ell\). The imposed relations make the representation trivial on \(H_p=I_p\cap\ker\overline{\mathcal R}\), where \(\overline{\mathcal R}\) is the residual representation. The tangent cocycle therefore vanishes there too. Since the residual image is finite, \(H_p\) has finite index in inertia.

The characteristic-zero averaging argument explains why this gives an inertia coboundary. For a cocycle on the resulting finite quotient \(\Gamma_p\), set \(S=\sum_{h\in\Gamma_p}\gamma(h)\) and \(n=|\Gamma_p|\). Summing the cocycle identity gives

\[ S=\sum_h\gamma(gh)=n\gamma(g)+g\cdot S, \qquad \gamma(g)=X_p-g\cdot X_p,\quad X_p=S/n. \]

Division by \(n\) is allowed in the characteristic-zero coefficient field. The class on inertia is zero. This still permits unramified variation of Frobenius; it does not say that the class vanishes on the entire decomposition group.

At \(\ell\). A permitted tangent lift factors through the prescribed local type ideal. The source's local deformation theorem shows that such dual-number points preserve the required crystalline structure, first on the subgroup fixing the sign and local type. Its dual-number comparison then identifies this condition with the finite condition on the adjoint cocycle. After the required descent, there is a period-valued \(z\) with

\[ z\in V_f\otimes_{\mathbb Q_\ell}B_{\mathrm{cris}}, \qquad \gamma(g)\otimes1=g\cdot z-z \quad(g\in G_{\mathbb Q_\ell}). \]

Intuitively, the local crystalline coordinates absorb the infinitesimal change. Fixed Hodge–Tate weights alone would not justify this step: it uses the local type ideal, the crystalline deformation result and the comparison for dual numbers. These local arguments establish membership in the Selmer space before any global vanishing theorem is used.

2.7.12.6.2 Derive the polarized action, including its minus sign.

Let \(c\) be the chosen complex conjugation and \(J\) the symmetric pairing matrix, with \(J^{-1}=J\). The source checks the identity

\[ r(g)^tJr(cgc^{-1})=\varepsilon(g)^{-2}J, \qquad g\in G_K. \]

\[ g\cdot X=r(g)Xr(g)^{-1}\quad(g\in G_K), \qquad c\cdot X=-JX^tJ. \]

\[ (1+\epsilon X)^{-1}=1-\epsilon X, \qquad J\bigl((1+\epsilon X)^{-1}\bigr)^tJ =1-\epsilon JX^tJ. \]

This is the linearized condition for preserving the polarization. It changes even the scalar matrices: \(g\cdot(aI)=aI\) for \(g\in G_K\), but \(c\cdot(aI)=-aI\). 2.7.12.6.3 Check that irreducibility survives the cyclotomic restriction.

An irreducible representation can become reducible on a smaller group. Here the source rules that out using the specified inertia eigenvalues. Put \(H=\ker\varepsilon\) and \(\Psi=\varepsilon^e\psi\), where \(e>0\) and \(\psi\) has finite order. If \(\rho|_H\) were reducible, the relevant Clifford-theory argument would produce a nontrivial self-twist:

\[ \varphi\ne1,\qquad H\subseteq\ker\varphi,\qquad \varphi^3=1,\qquad \rho\simeq\rho\otimes\varphi. \]

A self-twist means that multiplying every matrix by the character \(\varphi\) leaves its isomorphism class unchanged. In particular, it leaves its trace unchanged. On the inertia group \(I_\ell\), the imposed local condition gives

\[ \det(T-\rho(s)) =(T-\Psi(s))(T-1)(T-\Psi(s)^{-1}), \qquad s\in I_\ell. \]

Comparing traces with the self-twist therefore yields

\[ \begin{aligned} (\varphi(s)-1)\bigl(\Psi(s)+1+\Psi(s)^{-1}\bigr)&=0,\\ \varphi(s)\ne1 &\Longrightarrow \Psi(s)^2+\Psi(s)+1=0\\ &\Longrightarrow \Psi(s)^3=1. \end{aligned} \]

This would force a varying cyclotomic character to behave like a root of unity on too much inertia. The hypotheses make \(K/\mathbb Q\) unramified at \(\ell\), so the rational inertia group lies in \(G_K\), with cyclotomic image \(\mathbb Z_\ell^\times\). Since \(\varphi\) factors through \(\varepsilon\), choose \(s\in I_\ell\) with \(\varphi(s)\ne1\), and \(t\in I_\ell\) with \(\varepsilon(t)=1+\ell\).

If \(\varphi(t)\ne1\), the displayed implication gives \(\Psi(t)^3=1\). If \(\varphi(t)=1\), apply it to \(s\) and \(st\); dividing their equalities gives the same conclusion. Choose a positive integer \(d\) killing the finite-order character \(\psi\). Then

\[ 1=\Psi(t)^{3d}=(1+\ell)^{3ed}, \qquad e,d>0, \]

which is impossible: these are unequal rational integers embedded in a characteristic-zero field. This is not an argument about the \(\ell\)-adic norm, since \(|1+\ell|_\ell=1\). It proves \(\rho|_H\) irreducible. On \(H\), the twist by \(\varepsilon^{-1}\) is trivial, so \(r|_H\) is irreducible as required.

2.7.12.6.5 Understand what semisimple Hecke action rules out.

A Hecke operator acts on a space of automorphic forms; an eigenform records its eigenvalue. Collect the commuting operators relevant to the comparison into a commutative algebra \(\mathbb T_E\). For a finite-dimensional semisimple action, after extending the coefficient field to split the eigensystems, the image acts by simultaneous scalars:

\[ M=\bigoplus_i M_{\lambda_i}, \qquad Tm=\lambda_i(T)m \quad(m\in M_{\lambda_i}). \]

The same eigenvalue system may occur with multiplicity: \(\dim_E M_{\lambda_i}\) need not be one. What is excluded is a nontrivial Jordan block connecting eigenvectors. At a selected eigensystem \(\lambda\), an element whose eigenvalue is zero acts as zero on the entire localized space.

Here is an elementary model of the infinitesimal consequence. If a Hecke generator \(T\) satisfies a polynomial with distinct roots, then a proposed eigenvalue \(\alpha+\epsilon u\) must satisfy

\[ P(\alpha+\epsilon u) =P(\alpha)+\epsilon P'(\alpha)u=0. \qquad P'(\alpha)\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ u=0. \]

Once the eigenvalue \(\alpha\) is fixed, it cannot move infinitesimally. Equivalently, a split finite reduced eigenvalue algebra is a product of fields; localizing at one eigensystem selects one field, whose maximal ideal is zero.

2.7.12.6.6 Explain why an automorphic quotient alone is insufficient.

Knowing that automorphic representations give points of a deformation space does not show that they detect its infinitesimal structure. Even a surjection from a deformation ring to a reduced eigenvalue algebra is insufficient. For example,

\[ E[u]/(u^2)\twoheadrightarrow E,\qquad u\longmapsto0, \qquad (u)/(u^2)\simeq E. \]

The quotient sees one rigid eigenvalue system, while the source still has the tangent direction \(u\mapsto\epsilon\). This is the precise gap that a comparison theorem must close. It must rule out deformation directions invisible to the automorphic module, rather than merely produce an automorphic point.

For a surjection \(R\to\mathbb T\), tangent spaces run in the opposite direction:

\[ \operatorname{Hom}_E(\mathfrak m_{\mathbb T}/\mathfrak m_{\mathbb T}^2,E) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_E(\mathfrak m_R/\mathfrak m_R^2,E). \]

A zero space on the left places no upper bound on the right. The additional control supplied by patching is therefore essential.

\[ \mathcal L_{v,m}^{\perp} =\{z:\langle x,z\rangle_v=0 \text{ for every }x\in\mathcal L_{v,m}\}. \]

The global dual Selmer group consists of classes satisfying all these annihilator conditions:

\[ H^1_{\mathcal L^\perp}(F^+,W_m(1)) =\ker\!\left( H^1(F^+,W_m(1)) \longrightarrow \prod_v H^1(F_v^+,W_m(1))/\mathcal L_{v,m}^{\perp} \right). \]

It is a cohomology group with dual local conditions, not simply the vector-space dual of the original Selmer group. Global duality makes it the term controlling the excess in the deformation count. Our goal is to choose sets \(Q_N\), all of the same size, such that

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} H^1_{\mathcal L(Q_N)^\perp}(F^+,W_N(1)) \le C_{\mathrm{dual}}, \qquad C_{\mathrm{dual}}\ \text{independent of }N. \]
2.7.12.6.7.2 Understand why allowing ramification can shrink a dual group.

At a new prime \(v\), the original representation is unramified. Initially, the allowed local tangent classes are unramified too. Enlarge this condition by permitting one selected ramified direction. Orthogonality reverses the inclusion:

\[ \mathcal L^{\mathrm{old}}_{v,m} \subseteq\mathcal L^{\mathrm{new}}_{v,m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\mathcal L^{\mathrm{new}}_{v,m})^\perp \subseteq(\mathcal L^{\mathrm{old}}_{v,m})^\perp. \]

The elementary reason is that a functional which vanishes on a larger set has more equations to satisfy. In a vector-space model, if \(U'=U+Ea\), then

\[ (U')^\perp =\{\lambda\in U^\perp:\lambda(a)=0\}. \]

One added primal direction can remove one dual direction, provided the resulting test is nonzero on it. The choice of prime and Frobenius block is what makes that test useful. Arbitrary new primes would not guarantee any improvement.

2.7.12.6.7.3 Separate a Frobenius block without dividing modulo \(\varpi^m\).

Let \(v\) split in \(F\), choose a place \(\widetilde v\) above it, and put \(U_v=\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_{\widetilde v})\). Choose monic factors \(f_{v,1},f_{v,2}\in\mathcal O[T]\) of its characteristic polynomial that are coprime in \(E[T]\), equivalently with no common root in an algebraic closure of \(E\):

\[ \det(T-U_v)=f_{v,1}(T)f_{v,2}(T), \qquad \delta_v=\operatorname{Res}(f_{v,1},f_{v,2})\ne0. \]

The second factor selects the generalized eigenspaces on which ramification will be allowed. A Bézout identity gives integral polynomials \(A_v,B_v\) satisfying \(A_vf_{v,1}+B_vf_{v,2}=\delta_v\). Consequently

\[ P_v=\delta_v^{-1}A_v(U_v)f_{v,1}(U_v), \qquad D_v=\delta_vP_v=A_v(U_v)f_{v,1}(U_v) \in M_3(\mathcal O). \]

On the first block \(f_{v,1}(U_v)=0\), so \(P_v=0\). On the second, the Bézout identity gives \(P_v=1\). Thus \(P_v\) is a projector over \(E\). The integral operator \(D_v\) is the one we can safely reduce modulo \(\varpi^m\); it need not be idempotent:

\[ P_v^2=P_v,\qquad D_v^2=\delta_vD_v. \]

For example, \(f_{v,1}=T-\alpha\) and \(f_{v,2}=(T-\beta)^2\), with \(\alpha\ne\beta\), give \(\delta_v=(\alpha-\beta)^2\). Repeated eigenvalues inside the selected block are allowed. If \(\alpha-\beta\) is divisible by \(\varpi\), division by \(\delta_v\) is unavailable over \(\mathcal O/\varpi^m\). Keeping that factor is how the argument records the integral loss.

2.7.12.6.7.4 Compute the extra equation imposed on a dual class.

For a Taylor–Wiles prime of level \(N\), the residue-field size satisfies \(q_v\equiv1\pmod{\ell^N}\). Fix \(m\le N\), and let \(t_v:I_{\widetilde v}\to\mathbb Z_\ell\) be a surjective tame character. The modified local condition permits inertia cocycles of the form

\[ x(\tau)=a\,t_v(\tau)D_v\pmod{\varpi^m}, \qquad a\in\mathcal O/\varpi^m,\quad \tau\in I_{\widetilde v}. \]

More precisely, \(\mathcal L_{v,m}\) is the inverse image of the cyclic submodule generated by this inertia map. It includes every unramified class, since those restrict to zero on inertia. Its annihilator therefore consists of unramified dual classes with one additional constraint. Local Tate duality turns orthogonality to the displayed inertia direction into

\[ \mathcal L_{v,m}^{\perp} =\left\{[z]\in H^1_{\mathrm{unr}}(F_v^+,W_m(1)): \operatorname{tr}\!\left(D_vz(\mathrm{Frob}_{\widetilde v})\right) =0\pmod{\varpi^m}\right\}. \]

This is the detecting equation. It samples the cocycle at Frobenius, then measures its component on the selected block. Only its vanishing matters, so harmless unit factors from the normalization of local duality do not change the condition.

The test depends on the cohomology class, rather than on a chosen cocycle representative. Indeed, \(D_v\) commutes with \(U_v\), and the cyclotomic action of Frobenius is trivial modulo \(\varpi^m\). Adding a coboundary changes the trace by

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}\!\left(D_v(U_v XU_v^{-1}-X)\right) &=\operatorname{tr}\!\left((U_v^{-1}D_vU_v-D_v)X\right)\\ &=0. \end{aligned} \]

Notice that the condition is \(\operatorname{tr}(D_vz)=0\), with \(D_v=\delta_vP_v\). Cancelling a nonunit \(\delta_v\) here would incorrectly discard torsion classes.

2.7.12.6.7.5 Show why some block detects every surviving divisible direction.

Set \(F_\infty=F(\mu_{\ell^\infty})\) and \(H=\rho(G_{F_\infty})\). The cyclotomic irreducibility proved earlier supplies absolute irreducibility of this characteristic-zero image. After a suitable coefficient extension, semisimple elements of \(H\) span the full matrix algebra. This is the relevant form of the image-control argument.

Here is its linear-algebra content. If a nonzero matrix subspace \(U\subset M_3(E)\) were invisible to every generalized-eigenspace projector, then for \(Y\in U\),

\[ \operatorname{tr}(P_{h,\alpha}Y)=0\ \text{for all }h,\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{tr}(h^{\mathrm{ss}}Y) =\sum_\alpha\alpha\,\operatorname{tr}(P_{h,\alpha}Y)=0. \]

Spanning would then give \(\operatorname{tr}(AY)=0\) for every \(A\in M_3(E)\). Taking matrix units for \(A\) forces every entry of \(Y\) to vanish, a contradiction. Thus an appropriate block projector detects some nonzero component. No assumption of irreducibility modulo \(\ell\) enters this calculation.

Passing from matrices to global cocycles requires another input. A cohomological restriction lemma, using automorphic purity, ensures that a nonzero divisible family \(\kappa:E/\mathcal O\to H^1(G_{F^+,S},W_{E/\mathcal O}(1))\) remains nonzero on a subgroup with trivial adjoint and cyclotomic action. On that subgroup cocycles are homomorphisms and coboundaries vanish. Put \(T_{\mathcal O}=W_{\mathcal O}(1)\). Evaluation of the restricted family at \(\tau\) can be written

\[ \kappa(x)(\tau)=xY_\tau\bmod T_{\mathcal O}, \qquad Y_\tau\in T_{\mathcal O}, \qquad x\in E/\mathcal O. \]

The \(Y_\tau\) have a nonzero invariant \(E\)-span. Apply the matrix argument to these lattice elements, then clear denominators before evaluating a quotient-valued cocycle. For the resulting integral operator \(D=\delta P_{\sigma,\alpha}\),

\[ \operatorname{tr}(DY_\tau)\ne0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left[x\longmapsto \operatorname{tr}\!\left(D\kappa(x)(\tau)\right)\right] \ne0. \]

A nonzero element of \(\mathcal O\) acts nontrivially on the divisible module \(E/\mathcal O\), although it can kill particular finite-order values. This is why clearing denominators preserves a detecting family. An unscaled rational projector would not, in general, define an operator on \(W_{E/\mathcal O}(1)\).

One final cocycle calculation puts the detected value and the chosen block at the same group element. Let \(z\) represent a class from the family, with \(\operatorname{tr}(Dz(\tau))\ne0\). If \(\operatorname{tr}(Dz(\sigma))=0\), use

\[ z(\tau\sigma)=z(\tau)+\tau\cdot z(\sigma) =z(\tau)+z(\sigma). \]

Trivial adjoint action only says \(\rho(\tau)=aI\), not necessarily \(I\). Here \(a\in\mathcal O^\times\), because the representation is integral and invertible. Thus \(\rho(\tau\sigma)=a\rho(\sigma)\): the generalized eigenspaces and their projectors stay the same, while the eigenvalue \(\alpha\) becomes \(a\alpha\). The resultant-weighted operator changes only by a unit, so the trace test at \(\tau\sigma\) is still nonzero. If the test at \(\sigma\) was already nonzero, no replacement was needed.

2.7.12.6.7.6 Reduce the global problem to finitely many detecting equations.

Let \(S\) contain the coefficient-prime places and all ramified places. Write \(G_{F^+,S}\) for the Galois group unramified outside \(S\), and consider the larger group

\[ \mathcal D_\infty =H^1(G_{F^+,S},W_{E/\mathcal O}(1)). \]

Using this larger group avoids needing to predict which local Selmer restrictions will survive later. It is cofinitely generated over \(\mathcal O\): noncanonically it has a divisible part \((E/\mathcal O)^r\) and a finite-length remainder. The integer \(r\) is its corank.

Start with a nonzero divisible direction in the kernel of the tests already chosen. The preceding step finds another test which is nonzero on that direction. A nonzero endomorphism of \(E/\mathcal O\) is multiplication by a nonzero element of \(\mathcal O\); it is surjective with finite kernel. Thus each successful test lowers the remaining corank. After at most \(r\) such steps, the common kernel has finite length.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Lambda:\mathcal D_\infty&\longrightarrow(E/\mathcal O)^q,\\ [z]&\longmapsto \bigl(\operatorname{tr}(D_i z(\sigma_i))\bigr)_{i=1}^q, \qquad q\ge r,\\ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\ker\Lambda&<\infty. \end{aligned} \]

The \(\sigma_i\) lie in \(G_{F_\infty}\); this makes the cyclotomic twist trivial at these elements. The same trace calculation as in §2.7.12.6.7.4 makes each functional well-defined on classes. The number \(q\), the group elements, and their block factorizations are fixed before the precision \(N\) grows.

2.7.12.6.7.7 Replace the detecting group elements by Frobenius elements.

At each sufficiently large level \(N\), Chebotarev supplies distinct primes \(v_{N,1},\ldots,v_{N,q}\) outside \(S\), split in \(F\), whose Frobenius elements approximate the selected \(\sigma_i\). Their cyclotomic component is prescribed as well, giving

\[ q_{v_{N,i}}\equiv1\pmod{\ell^N}, \qquad \rho(\mathrm{Frob}_{\widetilde v_{N,i}}) \equiv\rho(\sigma_i)\pmod{\varpi^N}. \]

The first congruence provides a large tame \(\ell\)-power quotient of the residue-field units, on which the auxiliary inertia character can vary. The second approximates the matrix used in the detecting test. These are different coefficient scales: if \(e_E=v_\varpi(\ell)\), then

\[ \ell^N\mathcal O=\varpi^{e_EN}\mathcal O. \]

To explain the cocycle tests, retain more than matrix data in the finite Galois quotient used for Chebotarev. At a fixed level, the relevant global cohomology group is finite, so one can retain representatives of its finitely many classes together with the representation and cyclotomic character. Compatible choices of Frobenius representatives then reproduce the trace tests modulo \(\varpi^N\). Equal representation matrices alone would not imply equal cocycle evaluations.

The approximation is chosen sufficiently fine that Hensel's lemma also gives matching block factorizations,

\[ f_{v_{N,i},j}(T)\equiv f_{i,j}(T)\pmod{\varpi^N}, \qquad j=1,2. \]

When the resultant is a nonunit, obtaining these factor congruences can require extra initial precision. The proof absorbs that fixed loss before making the choice. The primes may change with \(N\); their number and the original detecting tests stay fixed.

2.7.12.6.7.8 Explain why the residual kernel stays bounded as \(N\) grows.

For the fixed factorization at \(\sigma_i\), let \(\delta_i\ne0\) be its resultant, and choose \(C_{\mathrm{res}}\ge\max_i v_\varpi(\delta_i)\). Since the resultant is polynomial in the coefficients, the factor congruences imply

\[ \delta_{N,i}\equiv\delta_i\pmod{\varpi^N}, \qquad N>C_{\mathrm{res}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\varpi(\delta_{N,i})=v_\varpi(\delta_i)\le C_{\mathrm{res}}. \]

Thus the projectors never demand a growing number of denominators. The finite-coefficient control argument also gives a bound independent of \(m\) for the kernels of the fixed tests

\[ \Lambda_m:H^1(G_{F^+,S},W_m(1)) \longrightarrow(\mathcal O/\varpi^m)^q. \]

This passage includes the cohomology of the coefficient sequence; it is not a claim that \(H^1(G,W_m(1))\) automatically equals the \(\varpi^m\)-torsion in \(H^1(G,W_{E/\mathcal O}(1))\). The invariant terms and the finite kernel left by the chosen tests contribute bounded errors.

A dual Selmer class for \(Q_N\) is unramified at each new prime, and its local condition is exactly the zero trace test computed above. After matching Frobenius and cocycle data, it therefore lies in the controlled kernel of \(\Lambda_N\). Retaining the other local conditions can only make the group smaller. Hence

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} H^1_{\mathcal L(Q_N)^\perp}(F^+,W_N(1)) \le C_{\mathrm{dual}}. \]

The conclusion is bounded length, not exact vanishing at every level. It says that increasing \(N\) does not uncover indefinitely many additional dual Selmer layers.

2.7.12.6.7.9 See bounded torsion in a two-dimensional model.

Consider two tests on \(D_N=(\mathcal O/\varpi^N)^2\):

\[ \Lambda_N(x,y)=(x,\varpi^a y), \qquad a\ge0\ \text{fixed}. \]

The first equation forces \(x=0\). The second permits those \(y\) killed by \(\varpi^a\). Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}D_N&=2N,\\ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\ker\Lambda_N&=\min(a,N)\le a. \end{aligned} \]

The underlying matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(1,\varpi^a)\) is invertible over \(E\), but one test has a fixed integral loss. This is the pattern the resultants allow. A second test \(y\mapsto0\) would leave \(N\) uncontrolled layers; a test \(y\mapsto\varpi^N y\) would do the same. Detecting every divisible direction and bounding the denominators are separate requirements, and the argument establishes both.

2.7.12.6.7.10 Turn dual control into a fixed number of deformation variables.

Each new local ramification condition adds a cyclic module of length at most \(m\) at level \(m\). The Greenberg–Wiles comparison, with the fixed original local conditions and their genericity hypotheses, gives an inequality of the form

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} H^1_{\mathcal L(Q_N)}(F^+,W_m) \le C_0+ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} H^1_{\mathcal L(Q_N)^\perp}(F^+,W_m(1)) +mq,\qquad 1\le m\le N. \]

The \(mq\) term accounts for the controlled directions deliberately introduced at the \(q\) auxiliary primes. Finite-level compatibility strengthens this length estimate to a generator statement:

\[ \mathcal O^q\longrightarrow H^1_{\mathcal L(Q_N)}(F^+,W_N), \qquad \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}\operatorname{coker} \le C_1+C_{\mathrm{dual}}. \]

One needs that compatibility argument: a total length bound alone would not identify \(q\) generators. Next, let \(R_N\) be the enhanced deformation algebra at level \(Q_N\) and \(P_N=\ker(R_N\to\mathcal O)\) its ideal at the chosen integral representation. The trace comparison relates Selmer classes to

\[ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O} (P_N/P_N^2,\mathcal O/\varpi^N). \]

Its kernel and cokernel are killed by a fixed power of \(\varpi\) times a power of the least common multiple of the resultants. In rank three that resultant exponent is \(3+3!=9\). The uniform resultant bound therefore makes this error uniform too. A separate uniform generator bound converts the bounded exponent into bounded length.

The resulting cotangent statement is

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal O[[x_1,\ldots,x_q]]\longrightarrow R_N, \qquad x_i\longmapsto y_{N,i}\in P_N,\\ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O} \frac{P_N}{P_N^2+(y_{N,1},\ldots,y_{N,q})} \le C_{\mathrm{cot}}, \qquad C_{\mathrm{cot}}\ \text{independent of }N. \end{gathered} \]

This supplies the fixed number of variables and bounded errors needed for patching. It does not yet prove the automorphic module is free: the module comparison and patching argument still produce that later input. Once freeness is available, §2.7.12.6.8 explains why semisimplicity forces the characteristic-zero Selmer group to vanish.

The role of the auxiliary primes. They turn selected Frobenius trace measurements into local dual Selmer equations. Image control makes the measurements detect every divisible direction; Chebotarev places them at actual primes; bounded resultants keep the integral losses fixed. The remaining dual group has bounded length, which gives uniform control of the deformation variables.

For the mathematical comparison theorem: Newton–Thorne, §2.4, for dual local conditions and finite-level control; Thorne, Lemma 5.5 and Proposition 5.6, for detecting elements and the bounded cotangent conclusion.

We now connect the bounded auxiliary errors to the faithful automorphic module. This is the patching mechanism in Thorne's adjoint-vanishing theorem. Keep the coefficient field \(E\), its integer ring \(\mathcal O\), and uniformizer \(\varpi\) fixed. 2.7.12.6.8.1 Keep both parameter rings.

For each precision \(N\), the auxiliary set \(Q_N\) has the same number \(q\) of primes. Its finite abelian \(\ell\)-group \(\Delta_N\) acts by changing the auxiliary level. Choose a quotient map from \(\mathbb Z_\ell^q\) for each \(N\), giving

\[ S_\infty=\mathcal O[[\mathbb Z_\ell^q]] \simeq\mathcal O[[t_1,\ldots,t_q]],\qquad \mathfrak a=(t_1,\ldots,t_q),\qquad S_\infty\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal O[\Delta_N]. \]

Here \(1+t_i\) is a chosen group generator. Setting all \(t_i=0\) makes the auxiliary characters trivial. The deformation ring \(R_N\) receives this action and also a map from \(R_\infty=\mathcal O[[x_1,\ldots,x_q]]\). The preceding cotangent estimate concerns the latter map; it says nothing by itself about flatness over \(S_\infty\).

There is also a finite algebra recording the chosen factorization of each Frobenius polynomial. Write \(\mathcal A_0\) for its coefficient algebra and \(\mathcal B_0\) for the algebra of the two factors, and put

\[ R_0=R(\varnothing)\otimes_{\mathcal A_0}\mathcal B_0,\qquad P_0=\ker(R_0\longrightarrow\mathcal O). \]

This marks the selected eigensystem together with its blocks. In characteristic zero the factors have nonzero resultant, so marking them is étale near that point: it adds a choice of block, not a new infinitesimal deformation.

2.7.12.6.8.2 Start with free level modules.

Let \(S_{N,1}\) be the localized space of integral automorphic forms at the deeper auxiliary level, and \(S_{N,0}\) the corresponding shallower space. At a sufficiently small fixed level, the level quotient acts freely on the relevant double-coset fibers. Functions on one free \(\Delta_N\)-orbit form a copy of its group algebra; the fixed coefficient lattice contributes additional copies. The resulting module statement and trace comparison are

\[ S_{N,1}\text{ is finite free over }\mathcal O[\Delta_N], \qquad S_{N,1}/\mathfrak aS_{N,1}\simeq S_{N,0}. \]

The trace map sums along a fiber. On functions on \(\Delta_N\), it sends a delta function to \(1\); after passing to coinvariants, this is an isomorphism. No division by \(|\Delta_N|\) is needed. That matters because \(|\Delta_N|\) is usually divisible by \(\ell\).

A second comparison relates \(S_{N,0}\) to the original-level forms with the Frobenius factorization adjoined. Its integral kernel and cokernel are controlled by resultants. These are the possible losses when two blocks are distinct in \(E\) but very close modulo \(\varpi\).

2.7.12.6.8.3 Patch finite quotients before taking the limit.

The sets \(Q_N\) change with \(N\), so the \(R_N\) are not a tower with canonical transition maps. Patching first compares their finite-precision data. To describe the construction, write

\[ \mathcal U((X_N)_N)= \mathbf O_{\mathcal F}\otimes_{\prod_N\mathcal O}\prod_N X_N. \]

Here \(\mathcal F\) is a nonprincipal ultrafilter, and \(\mathbf O_{\mathcal F}\) is the product ring localized at the idempotents of sets in \(\mathcal F\). This operation disregards any finite initial set of indices and selects coherent finite-precision behavior. It does not identify whole rings \(R_N\) with one another. With \(\mathfrak n=(\varpi,t_1,\ldots,t_q)\), the patched modules are

\[ \begin{aligned} M_1&=\varprojlim_m\mathcal U \bigl((S_{N,1}/\mathfrak n^mS_{N,1})_{N\ge m}\bigr),\\ M_0&=\varprojlim_m\mathcal U \bigl((S_{N,0}/\varpi^mS_{N,0})_{N\ge m}\bigr),\\ M&\simeq S_{\varnothing}\otimes_{\mathcal A_0}\mathcal B_0. \end{aligned} \]

For fixed \(m\), the sufficiently deep group algebras surject onto \(S_\infty/\mathfrak n^m\). Freeness at finite level therefore gives flat modules over this fixed Artinian ring. Products preserve flatness over a Noetherian ring, and the ultrafilter localization is a filtered localization. The truncations also satisfy

\[ M_{1,m+1}/\mathfrak n^mM_{1,m+1}\simeq M_{1,m}. \]

The adic flatness criterion now applies to this compatible inverse system. Together with the finite-level trace maps it gives

\[ M_1\text{ is }S_\infty\text{-flat},\qquad M_1/\mathfrak aM_1\simeq M_0,\qquad f:M\longrightarrow M_0. \]

These flatness and comparison statements are the module input to patching. Their finite-level and inverse-limit forms are developed in Newton–Thorne, §4.

One patches the rings using a filtration that makes these module actions continuous. For example, put \(u_N=\prod_{v\in Q_N}\operatorname{Res}_v^2\), and let \(J_N\) be the image of the maximal ideal of the unrestricted ring \(R_{S\cup Q_N}\). The relevant construction is

\[ R^{\mathrm p}=\varprojlim_m\mathcal U \bigl((R_N/(J_Nu_N)^m)_N\bigr), \qquad R^{\mathrm p}\twoheadrightarrow R_0. \]

The resultant factor in the filtration controls the comparison errors uniformly. The actions on \(M_1,M_0,M\) and the map \(f\) are compatible with this ring. The action on \(M\) factors through \(R_0\).

2.7.12.6.8.4 Remove the bounded cotangent error at the characteristic-zero point.

Let \(P^{\mathrm p}\) be the preimage of \(P_0\), and \(P_\infty=(x_1,\ldots,x_q)\). The patched ideal and its powers are obtained from the products of the corresponding \(P_N\) and their powers. Thus the uniform bound from §2.7.12.6.7.10 becomes

\[ \varpi^C\, \frac{P^{\mathrm p}} {(P^{\mathrm p})^2+(x_1,\ldots,x_q)} =0 \quad\text{for one fixed }C. \]

Localizing at \(P^{\mathrm p}\) inverts \(\varpi\). The remaining cotangent directions are therefore generated by the \(q\) images of \(x_i\). The complete-local comparison upgrades this to a surjection

\[ E[[x_1,\ldots,x_q]] \twoheadrightarrow B:=\widehat{(R^{\mathrm p})_{P^{\mathrm p}}}, \qquad \operatorname{edim}B\le q. \]

The algebra behind the upgrade is successive approximation: generators of the maximal ideal modulo its square generate each successive power modulo the next, so one lifts any element degree by degree and uses completeness. The patching proposition establishes the needed complete Noetherian local structure. The bounded error is used here, not as a substitute for the module flatness established above.

2.7.12.6.8.5 Make the module comparison exact near the selected point.

The patched element \(u=(u_N)_N\) kills \(\ker f\) and \(\operatorname{coker}f\). Its value at the selected eigensystem is nonzero: the fixed Frobenius factorizations have nonzero resultants, and the chosen congruences preserve them to arbitrarily high precision. Hence \(u\notin P^{\mathrm p}\), so localization makes \(u\) invertible. Consequently

\[ \mathfrak M:=M_{P_0}\simeq(M_0)_{P^{\mathrm p}}. \]

This explains why a uniformly bounded integral error is harmless at this point. One needs a common annihilator whose value there is nonzero; merely knowing that every individual finite-level error is finite would not give this conclusion.

Retain the first neighborhood of the trivial auxiliary character by setting

\[ \mathfrak M_1=(M_1/\mathfrak a^2M_1)_{P^{\mathrm p}}, \qquad \mathfrak M_1/\mathfrak a\mathfrak M_1\simeq\mathfrak M. \]

The module \(\mathfrak M\) is a nonzero finite-dimensional \(E\)-space: the original automorphic eigensystem survives at the selected point. Thus \(\mathfrak M_1\) is nonzero as well.

2.7.12.6.8.6 Use a square-zero neighborhood to obtain finite freeness.

After localizing the auxiliary ring at its augmentation ideal, its first neighborhood is

\[ A=(S_\infty)_{\mathfrak a}/\mathfrak a^2 \simeq E[t_1,\ldots,t_q]/(t_it_j:1\le i,j\le q). \]

Its maximal ideal \(\mathfrak n_A\) has square zero, and its embedding dimension is \(q\). Flatness of \(M_1\) passes to \(\mathfrak M_1\) over \(A\). This module is also finite: lift an \(E\)-basis of \(\mathfrak M=\mathfrak M_1/\mathfrak n_A\mathfrak M_1\) to a submodule \(L\). Then

\[ \mathfrak M_1=L+\mathfrak n_A\mathfrak M_1 =L+\mathfrak n_A L+\mathfrak n_A^2\mathfrak M_1=L. \]

A finite flat module over a local ring is free. In particular, for \(h=\dim_E\mathfrak M>0\),

\[ \mathfrak M_1\simeq A^h,\qquad \dim_E\mathfrak M_1=(q+1)h. \]

Keeping \(\mathfrak a^2\), rather than immediately setting \(\mathfrak a=0\), retains the independent first-order level directions needed by the next criterion.

2.7.12.6.8.7 Transfer freeness to the deformation algebra.

The localized deformation-ring action on \(\mathfrak M_1\) extends to its completion \(B\). To see why, \(\mathfrak M_1\) is finite-dimensional over \(E\), so the image of the local ring in \(\operatorname{End}_E(\mathfrak M_1)\) is a finite-dimensional local algebra. Its maximal ideal is nilpotent; a power of \(P^{\mathrm p}\) therefore kills \(\mathfrak M_1\), and the action factors through that same finite quotient of the completion.

Write \(B_1=B/\mathfrak a^2B\). We have a local map \(A\to B_1\), and \(\mathfrak M_1\) is a finite \(B_1\)-module that is free over \(A\). The number of available deformation generators gives

\[ \operatorname{edim}B_1 \le\operatorname{edim}B \le q=\operatorname{edim}A. \]

The precise freeness criterion. For a local map of Noetherian local rings \(A\to B_1\), a nonzero finite \(B_1\)-module that is flat over \(A\) is flat over \(B_1\) if \(\operatorname{edim}B_1\le\operatorname{edim}A\). This is Brochard's Theorem 1.1. Finiteness over the local ring then makes the module free.

All three requirements now have separate justifications: nonzero automorphic forms, flatness from auxiliary levels, and an embedding-dimension bound from dual Selmer control. The criterion yields

\[ \mathfrak M_1\simeq B_1^d,\quad d>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathfrak M\simeq(B/\mathfrak aB)^d. \]

Intuitively, the automorphic module already accommodates every independent first-order level parameter without a relation. The deformation algebra has no larger supply of generators. The criterion is what turns this numerical match and flatness into the assertion that the module has no hidden relations over that algebra.

2.7.12.6.8.8 Return to the original deformation problem.

Put \(R=\widehat{(R_0)_{P_0}}\). Trivial auxiliary characters give a surjection \(B/\mathfrak aB\twoheadrightarrow R\), through which the action on \(\mathfrak M\) factors. If \(I\) is its kernel, then \(I\mathfrak M=0\). But the displayed free module has positive rank, so

\[ I\mathfrak M=0,\quad \mathfrak M\simeq(B/\mathfrak aB)^d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I=0. \]

Indeed, applying \(a\in I\) to the first basis vector leaves \(a\) as its first coordinate. Thus \(a=0\). We have proved the required comparison

\[ B/\mathfrak aB\simeq R,\qquad \mathfrak M\simeq R^d,\quad d>0. \]

The module comparison, the cotangent presentation, and the application of the criterion are assembled in Thorne, §6.7, Propositions 6.10–6.12. This explains the literature theorem used here; it does not identify a particular internal Lean patching implementation.

2.7.12.6.8.9 Combine faithfulness with semisimplicity.

Freeness gives \(\operatorname{Ann}_R(\mathfrak M)=0\). At the original level, the characteristic-zero Hecke action is semisimple. Adjoining the marked factors preserves this at \(P_0\), because their nonzero resultants make the extension étale there. After localization at the chosen eigensystem, every simple constituent is acted on through the same residue character \(R\to E\). Therefore the maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m_R\) kills the whole semisimple module:

\[ \mathfrak m_R\mathfrak M=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathfrak m_R\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_R(\mathfrak M)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R=E. \]

The tangent comparison from the previous sections, unchanged by the étale marking of the factors, now gives

\[ H^1_{\mathrm{BK}}(F^+,W_E) \simeq\operatorname{Hom}_E(\mathfrak m_R/\mathfrak m_R^2,E)=0. \]

This closes the chain: auxiliary primes control generators; automorphic level modules supply flatness; patching and the freeness criterion make the action faithful; semisimplicity removes the maximal ideal. Integral torsion can still remain before inverting \(\varpi\), which is why the later length estimate is necessary.

2.7.12.6.9 Transfer the conclusion to the tangent used in the trace bound.

Vanishing after the auxiliary field reductions descends to the original field. For a finite extension \(F^+/\mathbb Q\), restriction preserves the local Selmer conditions and corestriction satisfies

\[ \operatorname{cor}\circ\operatorname{res} =[F^+:\mathbb Q]\operatorname{id} \quad\text{on }H^1(\mathbb Q,V_E). \]

The degree is invertible in \(E\), so restriction is injective; a class whose restriction vanishes was already zero. Together with the coefficient-field comparison, this gives vanishing over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\). We must still identify \(V\) with the original polarized adjoint \(V_f\). On \(G_K\), a scalar twist cancels:

\[ (\varepsilon(g)^{-1}\rho(g))X (\varepsilon(g)^{-1}\rho(g))^{-1} =\rho(g)X\rho(g)^{-1}. \]

The pairing calculation in §2.7.12.6.2 also matches the action at \(c\). Since \(G_K\) and \(c\) generate \(G_{\mathbb Q}\), this identifies the full polarized representations, and hence their local conditions. For our original tangent cocycle \(\gamma\),

\[ [\gamma]\in H^1_{\mathrm{BK}}(\mathbb Q,V_f)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \exists X,\ \gamma(g)=X-g\cdot X \quad\text{for all }g\in G_{\mathbb Q}. \]

The same \(X\) works globally. This is exactly the change-of-basis statement needed in §2.7.12.8, where normalization restricts \(X\) to the grading direction. Vanishing of the unframed tangent does not remove those basis-change directions from a framed parameter space.

What has vanished? The characteristic-zero Selmer space measuring permitted deformations up to change of basis. Integral congruence layers can remain: \(\mathcal O/\varpi^m\mathcal O\) becomes zero after tensoring with \(E\), despite having length \(m\). That is why the later cotangent-to-trace estimate still has work to do.

Selective source checks: the cyclotomic irreducibility argument and the rank-three application, including the twist and polarized action.

2.7.12.7 Control all four pieces in the rank-two case.

A Type II point is not covered by the preceding irreducible rank-three argument. Its outer block extends to an odd rank-two representation \(W\) of \(G_{\mathbb Q}\), while the middle line is fixed. The source's modularity input supplies a newform \(h\) of weight \(2e+1\), with \(e\ge1\), such that \(W\otimes\varepsilon_\ell^{-e}\) is attached to \(h\). The determinant of \(W\) is the quadratic character \(\eta\).

The full adjoint contains more than endomorphisms of \(W\): it also contains maps between the outer block and the middle line. With the polarized action included, the source separates its nine dimensions into

\[ V_f\simeq A_W\oplus W\oplus(W\otimes\eta)\oplus C, \qquad 9=4+2+2+1. \]

Here \(A_W\) is the polarized rank-two adjoint corner, the two rank-two summands describe mixing with the middle line, and \(C\) is the middle-coordinate piece with the quadratic-sign action. Each requires its own vanishing input:

PieceWhy its Selmer class vanishes
\(A_W\)The newform-to-automorphic comparison and the polarized rank-two adjoint-vanishing theorem.
\(W\)The source's Kato-type input for the specified positive twist of the newform representation.
\(W\otimes\eta\)The same input with the permitted finite-conductor quadratic twist.
\(C\)The split-representation vanishing argument in nonpositive grading weight, applied in weight zero.

The Kato-type input retains the odd coefficient-prime, positive twist, newform attachment and finite-character hypotheses. It does not assert vanishing for every modular representation or every twist.

The decomposition also explains how the four conclusions combine. Its projections \(p_i\) are equivariant, so they carry a global cocycle and its local splitting witnesses to cocycles and witnesses in each piece. If \(p_i(\gamma(g))=X_i-g\cdot X_i\), the equivariant reconstruction maps combine the \(X_i\) into one \(X\), giving

\[ \gamma(g)=X-g\cdot X \qquad\text{for every }g\in G_{\mathbb Q}. \]
See how the two off-diagonal pieces are separated.

Using matrix indices \(0,1,2\), the projections are

\[ p_A(X)=\begin{pmatrix}X_{00}&X_{02}\\X_{20}&X_{22}\end{pmatrix}, \quad p_+(X)=\binom{X_{01}+X_{12}}{X_{21}-X_{10}}, \quad p_-(X)=\binom{X_{01}-X_{12}}{X_{21}+X_{10}}. \]

Let \(p_C(X)=X_{11}E_{11}\), where \(E_{11}\) has its only nonzero entry in the middle. The reconstruction maps put these coordinates back into their matrix positions, with

\[ X=s_Ap_A(X)+\tfrac12s_+p_+(X) +\tfrac12s_-p_-(X)+p_C(X). \]

The plus and minus combinations distinguish how the mixing terms transform under complex conjugation. The factors \(1/2\) undo the sum-and-difference projections. Equivariance means this identity works for cocycles and coboundaries, not just for their underlying vector spaces.

Modularity of the outer block is therefore one input to full rigidity. Vanishing for its adjoint corner alone would leave the off-diagonal extension classes uncontrolled.

2.7.12.8 Show that normalization leaves only the grading orbit.

We now know that every allowed tangent cocycle at a two-sided point is some \(\gamma_X\). But not every infinitesimal basis change respects the chosen normalization. The source fixes the matrix part at a distinguished complex conjugation \(c_\infty\) and imposes two further marked-entry conditions. Linearizing those conditions restricts \(X\).

At \(c_\infty\), the requirement \(\gamma_X(c_\infty)=0\) gives \(X=-JX^{\mathsf T}J\). The two other conditions become commutator equations at the marked element. Using the invertible marked difference and invertibility of \(2\), the source's matrix calculation gives precisely

\[ \gamma_X\text{ respects the normalization} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X=aH,\qquad H=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,-1),\quad a\in\kappa. \]

This is the infinitesimal form of the surviving torus \(\operatorname{diag}(t,1,t^{-1})\). All other permitted coordinate motions have been removed. On a homogeneous model function \(b\in A_j\), its action and derivative are

\[ (t\cdot\xi)(b)=t^j\xi(b),\qquad D_{\mathrm{gr}}(b)= \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=1}t^j\xi(b)=j\,\xi(b). \]

There is one further logical step: a tangent cocycle must faithfully record the model derivation. The source proves this for the model and its normalized deformation ring. For derivations \(D,D'\) represented by lifts \(F,F'\), it gives

\[ a\,\gamma_F+b\,\gamma_{F'}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad aD+bD'=0. \]

Since the cocycles are multiples of \(\gamma_H\), any two such derivations are linearly dependent. At a two-sided point, \(D_{\mathrm{gr}}\) is nonzero and kills the homogeneous equation ideal. Thus

\[ \operatorname{Der}_{\mathcal O,\xi}(A/I,\kappa) =\kappa D_{\mathrm{gr}}. \]

There is exactly one tangent direction in these coordinates, but it only rescales a basis. This is the precise meaning of rigidity transverse to the grading orbit.

2.7.12.9 Keep the split point and one-sided loci separate.

The split base point is not two-sided, and its rigidity is proved by a different argument. The source needs vanishing only for Bloch–Kato cocycles supported in matrix grading weights \(j\le0\). That argument uses its character-cohomology, local and global inputs; it is not an application of irreducible rank-three automorphy to a reducible representation.

After this vanishing result makes a cocycle a coboundary, normalization again forces \(X\in\kappa H\). At the split diagonal representation \(\mathcal R_0\), however, the grading torus fixes the point. Consequently,

\[ \gamma_H(g)=H-g\cdot H=0,\qquad \gamma_F=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad D=0 \quad\text{in the required nonpositive weights}. \]

This supplies rational spanning by the equations in the weights \(-1,-2,0\). For the selected cotangent quotient it permits

\[ C_{-1}\otimes_{\mathcal O}E=0, \qquad E=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O), \]

while its integral length can still be positive. For example, \((\mathcal O/\varpi^n\mathcal O)\otimes_{\mathcal O}E=0\), although its length is \(n\). Characteristic-zero rigidity removes free directions; it does not measure how many integral torsion layers remain.

The source also separately proves that a normalized point lying in both triangular loci is the split reference point. This supplies the condition controlling one-sided degeneracies in the graded support argument. The three jobs are distinct: nonpositive-weight rigidity at the base, identification of the doubly triangular point, and rigidity at two-sided points.

2.7.12.10 Remove the orbit direction and obtain multiplicity one.

We can now explain the multiplicity bound used in §2.7.13.10. At a two-sided point, choose nonvanishing functions of weights \(-2\) and \(+2\). After extending the generic coefficient field to \(\kappa\), move along the grading orbit: choose \(t\) with \(t^2=\xi(b_-)\), so that \((t\cdot\xi)(b_-)=t^{-2}\xi(b_-)=1\). Keeping the same homogeneous functions, take the local slice

\[ b_-=1,\qquad b_+\ne0, \qquad b_-\in A_{-2},\quad b_+\in A_2. \]

A tangent to this slice must satisfy \(D(b_-)=0\). Since every tangent is \(aD_{\mathrm{gr}}\),

\[ 0=D(b_-)=a(-2)\xi(b_-)=-2a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=0. \]

Fixing the value of \(b_-\) has removed the last change-of-basis direction. The source uses its finiteness hypotheses to turn this into a finite, reduced generic slice. The local algebra explanation is useful: at a finite local algebra over the algebraically closed coefficient field, the tangent space is dual to \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\). If it is zero, Nakayama's lemma forces \(\mathfrak m=0\); the point has no nilpotent thickening. This reasoning concerns the generic slice, after inverting \(\varpi\), not the entire integral deformation ring.

Next let \(B=A_0\), let \(\tau:B\to\kappa\) be a surviving two-sided degree-zero character, and put \(\mathfrak p=\ker\tau\). The slice and orbit calculations show that each localized weight piece

\[ L_w=\bigl((A/I)_w\bigr)_{\mathfrak p} \]

is cyclic and is annihilated by the maximal ideal of \(B_{\mathfrak p}\). Intuitively, a function of fixed weight along one orbit is determined by a single coefficient; the reduced slice contributes no additional infinitesimal layers. Algebraically, the two properties give a surjection from the residue field:

\[ \kappa(\mathfrak p)\twoheadrightarrow L_w, \qquad \operatorname{length}_{B_{\mathfrak p}}L_w\le1. \]

This is the exact starting bound needed in the next subsection. Replacing the selected \(u+1+v\) equations by their squares can multiply this bound by at most \(2^{u+1+v}\). The lower cotangent count contributes \(2^{u+v}\), and their ratio leaves the factor of two:

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Selmer vanishing}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{normalized orbit rigidity}\ \Longrightarrow\ \operatorname{length}_{B_{\mathfrak p}}L_w\le1,\\ 2^{u+v}\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1} \le 2^{u+1+v}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal T} v_\varpi\!\left(\xi(a_\sigma)\right). \end{gathered} \]

The full graded comparison in §2.7.13 is still needed for the second line. Rigidity supplies its multiplicity input; it does not by itself produce a numerical integral bound or force \(C_{-1}=0\).

What each input contributes. Local deformation conditions put the tangent class in the correct Selmer space. Automorphy and modularity make the relevant vanishing theorems applicable, separately in the two cases. Vanishing makes the tangent a change of basis; normalization leaves the grading orbit; the slice and weight-piece arguments give multiplicity at most one. The later valuation comparison measures the integral congruence depth that these characteristic-zero statements leave open.

Two source entry points for this passage: the assembly of local Selmer conditions and point rigidity; the passage from normalized coboundaries to the one-dimensional derivation condition.

2.7.13.1 State the precise comparison and its hypotheses.

Keep the coefficient discrete valuation ring \(\mathcal O\), uniformizer \(\varpi\), and selected cotangent quotient from §§2.7.10–11. Write \(E=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O)\), let \(\overline E\) be an algebraic closure, and normalize \(v_\varpi(\varpi)=1\). It vanishes at the split base point \(x\). 2.7.13.2 Separate a change of basis from a genuine deformation.

For a homogeneous function \(b\in A_j\), the grading torus acts by

\[ (t\cdot f)(b)=t^j f(b),\qquad t\in\overline E^\times. \]

Trace has weight zero, so \(f(a)\) is constant along an orbit. The orbit itself has an infinitesimal direction: substitute \(t=1+\epsilon\), with \(\epsilon^2=0\), to obtain

\[ (1+\epsilon)^j f(b)=f(b)+\epsilon\,j f(b), \qquad D_{\mathrm{gr}}(b)=j f(b). \]

The Selmer-vanishing and normalization argument in §2.7.12.8 shows that permitted derivations killing the equations span at most one dimension. At a two-sided point, the displayed grading derivation is nonzero in characteristic zero. Thus the surviving direction is accounted for by this change of basis; there is no additional transverse infinitesimal motion.

This does not mean that traces directly detect the cocycles of §2.7.7. Those cocycles enter through off-diagonal matrices \(M(t)\), and

\[ \operatorname{tr}\!\left((1+\epsilon M(t))\rho_0(g)\right) =\operatorname{tr}\rho_0(g) +\epsilon\,\operatorname{tr}\!\left(M(t)\rho_0(g)\right) =\operatorname{tr}\rho_0(g). \]

The first-order trace is unchanged. The proof instead compares the total integral length of these directions with how closely the other, rigid orbits approach the split trace.

2.7.13.3 See why a valuation measures a module's length.

Start with a finite torsion-free domain \(D\) over \(\mathcal O\) and a nonzero \(b\in D\). A finite torsion-free module over a discrete valuation ring is free. In an \(\mathcal O\)-basis, multiplication \(m_b:D\to D\) is an injective square matrix. Smith normal form gives elementary divisors \(\varpi^{n_1},\ldots,\varpi^{n_d}\), up to units. Consequently,

\[ D/bD\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal O/\varpi^{n_i}\mathcal O, \qquad \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}(D/bD) =\sum_i n_i=v_\varpi(\det m_b). \]

The determinant is the algebra norm of \(b\). Characteristic zero makes the generic field extension separable, so the norm becomes a product over its embeddings into \(\overline E\). Taking valuations turns that product into a sum:

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}(D/bD) =v_\varpi\!\left(N_{D/\mathcal O}(b)\right) =\sum_{\tau:D\to\overline E}v_\varpi\!\left(\tau(b)\right). \]

Each embedding is counted. In particular, repeated numerical values are not discarded. This elementary index calculation is the reason that trace valuations can eventually appear on the right side of a length bound. The remaining task is to construct a module to which this calculation can be applied with controlled multiplicities.

2.7.13.4 Use the quotient length minus the kernel length.

Let \(B=A_0\), the weight-zero ring. Multiplication by a degree-zero element \(a\) on a module \(N\) need not be injective. When its kernel and cokernel have finite length, define

\[ N[a]=\{z\in N:az=0\},\qquad \Theta_a(N)=\operatorname{length}_B(N/aN) -\operatorname{length}_B(N[a]). \]

The subtraction makes this quantity additive in exact sequences. It also removes a misleading contribution from torsion. If \(T\) already has finite length, the multiplication sequence gives

\[ 0\longrightarrow T[a]\longrightarrow T \xrightarrow{\ a\ }T\longrightarrow T/aT\longrightarrow0, \qquad \Theta_a(T)=0. \]

For example, if \(aT=0\), both the kernel and the quotient are all of \(T\). Counting only the quotient would add \(\operatorname{length}T\), even though such torsion disappears in characteristic zero. The correction cancels it exactly.

For a list \(\mathbf t\) of homogeneous equations, the argument iterates this quotient-minus-kernel operation. Write \(\chi(\mathbf t;N)_{\mathbf n}\) for the resulting graded Euler characteristic. If \(t\) has multidegree \(\delta\), its recursion is

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi(\varnothing;N)_{\mathbf n}&=\operatorname{length}_B N_{\mathbf n},\\ \chi((\delta,t),\mathbf u;N)_{\mathbf n} &=\chi(\mathbf u;N/tN)_{\mathbf n} -\chi(\mathbf u;N[t](-\delta))_{\mathbf n}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(N(-\delta)_{\mathbf n}=N_{\mathbf n-\delta}\) keeps the multiplication map homogeneous. This is the familiar Euler-characteristic bookkeeping behind Koszul complexes. The source proves the finiteness, additivity, order-independence and eventual positivity needed in this setting. All comparisons below take place in sufficiently large multidegrees; one cannot freely drop kernel terms or assume positivity without those hypotheses.

We now describe the special model, rather than an arbitrary ring. Use \(u,v,\alpha,\beta\) for its coordinate counts; these are unrelated to the arithmetic twist parameter \(r\). After eliminating the weight-zero chart coordinates with their equations, the remaining formal coordinates and equations have this pattern:

2.7.13.6 Pass to leading terms while retaining integral depth.

The smooth chart is a map \(\phi:A\to E[[\text{weighted coordinates}]]\). Let \(J\subseteq\mathscr R\) be the ideal of Rees expressions whose coefficients map to zero under \(\phi\). The source constructs a larger homogeneous ideal \(\mathfrak q\) that discards terms above the leading negative-weight contribution in each Rees degree.

In degree \(\mathbf n\), that leading contribution is \(2n_0+4n_2\), and the positive contribution is \(2n_1+2n_2\). The quotient \(\overline P=\mathscr R/\mathfrak q\) is torsion-free over \(\mathcal O\). It retains exactly the scalar depth needed here:

\[ F_i^2=\varpi^{2c_i}y_i^2 \quad\text{in the leading quotient},\qquad J\subseteq\mathfrak q. \]

The degree-\(e_0\) Rees labels are understood in this equality. It uses the saturated quadratic congruence from the previous step and is a proved property of the leading-term construction. Weight-zero functions vanishing at \(x\) also map into \(\mathfrak q\); that fact will allow the trace cut to be compared with this quotient.

2.7.13.7 Extract one contribution for each power of the uniformizer.

Set

\[ H(\mathbf n)=\operatorname{length}_B (\overline P/\varpi\overline P)_{\mathbf n}, \qquad \Delta_\delta H(\mathbf n)=H(\mathbf n)-H(\mathbf n-\delta). \]

A finite difference is the counting effect of cutting a homogeneous direction. For example, quotienting a graded vector space by an injective multiplication map of degree \(\delta\) subtracts the dimension at \(\mathbf n-\delta\) from the dimension at \(\mathbf n\). Euler characteristics retain the same bookkeeping when kernels are present.

The source's scalar-factor identity says, under its finiteness conditions,

\[ \chi((\delta,\varpi^n y),\mathbf u;\overline P)_{\mathbf n} =n\,\Delta_{\deg\mathbf u}H(\mathbf n) +\chi((\delta,y),\mathbf u;\overline P)_{\mathbf n}. \]

In this formula \(n\) is a scalar exponent, \(\mathbf n\) is the multidegree, and \(\Delta_{\deg\mathbf u}\) is the product of differences for all remaining sections. Removing one factor of \(\varpi\) exposes one special-fiber layer. Removing \(n\) such factors exposes \(n\) layers, which explains addition of the exponents.

Apply this identity successively to the factors \(\varpi^{2c_i}\) in \(F_i^2\). Eventual positivity bounds the remaining Euler characteristic below by zero. There are \(u-1+\alpha\) remaining sections of degree \(e_0\), \(v+\beta\) of degree \(e_1\), and one of degree \(e_2\). Thus

\[ \chi(\mathbf t;\overline P)_{\mathbf n} \ge 2\Bigl(\sum_i c_i\Bigr) \Delta_{e_0}^{\,u-1+\alpha} \Delta_{e_1}^{\,v+\beta} \Delta_{e_2}H(\mathbf n). \]

The module's torsion depth now appears as the explicit factor \(2\lambda\). It remains to calculate the finite-difference factor.

2.7.13.8 Calculate the lower constant by counting monomials.

The leading-term comparison identifies \(H(\mathbf n)\) with a monomial count. Put \(P=2n_0+4n_2\) and \(Q=2n_1+2n_2\). A variable of negative weight \(-1\) contributes \(1+U+U^2+\cdots\); one of weight \(-2\) contributes \(1+U^2+U^4+\cdots\). The positive variables are counted similarly with \(V\). Therefore

\[ H(\mathbf n)=[U^P V^Q]\,\mathcal H(U,V),\qquad \mathcal H(U,V)= \frac{1}{(1-U)^u(1-U^2)^\alpha (1-V)^{v+1}(1-V^2)^{\beta+1}}. \]

The notation \([U^P V^Q]\) means “take that coefficient.” Decreasing \(n_0\) by one decreases \(P\) by two, so \(\Delta_{e_0}\) corresponds to multiplying the generating series by \(1-U^2\). Similarly, \(\Delta_{e_1}\) gives \(1-V^2\), and \(\Delta_{e_2}\) gives \(1-U^4V^2\). The series for the required differences simplifies to

\[ \begin{aligned} &(1-U^2)^{u-1+\alpha}(1-V^2)^{v+\beta} (1-U^4V^2)\mathcal H(U,V)\\ &\qquad= \frac{(1+U)^{u-1}(1+V)^v(1-U^4V^2)} {(1-U)(1-V)(1-V^2)}. \end{aligned} \]

Use \(1-U^4V^2=(1-V^2)+V^2(1-U^4)\) to split this expression into

\[ \frac{(1+U)^{u-1}(1+V)^v}{(1-U)(1-V)} + \frac{V^2(1+U)^{u-1}(1+V)^v(1+U+U^2+U^3)} {(1-V)(1-V^2)}. \]

The second term is a polynomial in \(U\), so it contributes nothing once \(P\) exceeds its degree. In the first term, sufficiently large \(P,Q\) collect every coefficient of the numerator. Their sum is its value at \(U=V=1\), namely \(2^{u-1+v}\). Hence, for sufficiently large multidegrees,

\[ \Delta_{e_0}^{\,u-1+\alpha} \Delta_{e_1}^{\,v+\beta}\Delta_{e_2}H(\mathbf n) =2^{u-1+v}, \qquad \chi(\mathbf t;\overline P)_{\mathbf n} \ge 2^{u+v}\lambda. \]

This calculation explains the lower power of two. The parameters \(\alpha,\beta\) cancel because the weight-two equations take exactly the corresponding finite differences. The extra positive coordinates and the nonlinear section determine the constant that remains.

2.7.13.9.1 Choose a trace representative that is injective before the equation cuts.

We first arrange

\[ a'-a\in I,\qquad a'\in A_0,\qquad x(a')=0, \qquad \phi(a')\ne0. \]

If \(\phi(a)\ne0\), take \(a'=a\). Otherwise, the nonzero negative cotangent direction and spanning by the \(F_i\) imply that some \(F_i\) has \(\phi(F_i)\ne0\). Indeed, if every \(\phi(F_i)\) vanished, every linear coefficient they span would vanish, contradicting the chart's negative coordinate. The chart also has a positive coordinate, so choose \(g\in A_1\) with \(\phi(g)\ne0\). Then

\[ a'=a+gF_i,\qquad gF_i\in I\cap A_0,\qquad \phi(a')=\phi(g)\phi(F_i)\ne0. \]

The product has weight zero, and its base value is zero because nonzero-weight functions vanish at the base point. For every solution \(f\) of \(I\), we still have \(f(a')=f(a)\). Thus this change preserves exactly the trace valuations used in the final bound.

The ideal \(J\) consists of Rees expressions whose coefficients vanish under \(\phi\). If \(a'p\in J\), applying \(\phi\) coefficient by coefficient gives \(\phi(a')\phi(p_{\mathbf n})=0\). The power-series target is a domain, so every \(\phi(p_{\mathbf n})=0\), and \(p\in J\). Consequently,

\[ \mathcal P[a']=0, \qquad \chi((a'),\mathbf t;\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n} =\chi(\mathbf t;\mathcal P/a'\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n}. \]

This proves injectivity at this stage. Later quotients can acquire \(a'\)-torsion, which the final Euler characteristic will retain with a minus sign.

2.7.13.9.2 State the condition that makes the eventual lengths finite.

Let \(\mathfrak b\) be the maximal ideal of the local ring \(B=A_0\). In the standard \(\mathbb N^3\)-graded Rees algebra define

\[ \mathfrak r= (\mathscr R_{e_0})(\mathscr R_{e_1})(\mathscr R_{e_2}). \]

Each parenthesis denotes the ideal generated by that homogeneous piece. A power of \(\mathfrak r\) requires positive degree in all three directions. For a finitely generated graded module \(N\) and a list of homogeneous sections \(\mathbf s\), the required admissibility condition is

\[ \exists L\ge0:\qquad \bigl((\mathfrak b\mathscr R)\mathfrak r\bigr)^L \subseteq \operatorname{Ann}_{\mathscr R}(N)+(\mathbf s). \]

This condition survives each step of the Euler recursion. If the next section is \(s\), both \(N/sN\) and \(N[s]\) are annihilated by \(\operatorname{Ann}(N)+(s)\). We can therefore remove \(s\) from the list and use the same containment for either resulting module. Degree shifts preserve annihilators.

At the end of any branch, no sections remain. The terminal module \(V\) satisfies \(\mathfrak b^L\mathfrak r^L V=0\). Choose finitely many homogeneous generators of \(V\). Once every coordinate of \(\mathbf n\) exceeds their degrees by at least \(L\), standard grading gives

\[ V_{\mathbf n}\subseteq\mathfrak r^L V, \qquad \mathfrak b^L V_{\mathbf n}=0. \]

Each graded piece is a finite \(B\)-module. Its filtration by powers of \(\mathfrak b\) now has finitely many layers, each finite-dimensional over \(B/\mathfrak b\); hence its length is finite. The recursion has finitely many branches, so one componentwise lower bound on \(\mathbf n\) works for all of them. Intermediate modules need not themselves have finite-length graded pieces before the remaining cuts are made.

2.7.13.9.3 Explain why this model satisfies admissibility.

For the present comparison, the source establishes the concrete containment

\[ \bigl((\mathfrak b\mathscr R)\mathfrak r\bigr)^L \subseteq J+(\mathbf t,a'). \]

The proof tests prime ideals containing the right side. A prime containing \(\mathfrak r\) already contains the product on the left. At a prime not containing \(\mathfrak r\), each Rees direction survives. The equations then force its contraction to \(B\) to contain the degree-zero part of \(I\). Finiteness of the degree-zero quotient gives two possibilities: the contraction is \(\mathfrak b\), or it is the kernel of a characteristic-zero character \(\tau:B\to\overline E\).

In the second case, suppose some product \(fg\), with \(f\in A_{-2}\) and \(g\in A_2\), survives under \(\tau\). The graded point-lifting argument extends \(\tau\) to a two-sided solution \(\xi\) of \(I\). Since the prime also contains \(a'\), this would give

\[ \xi(I)=0,\qquad \xi\text{ two-sided},\qquad \xi(a')=\tau(a')=0, \]

contradicting the trace-nonvanishing hypothesis at two-sided points. If every such product vanishes, the model's separate control of the base point and one-sided loci applies: surviving Rees directions would produce positive and negative one-sided lifts with the same degree-zero character. The one-sided separation condition excludes their coexistence away from the base; at the base, the negative cotangent-spanning condition excludes the negative lift. Thus a relevant prime over all the cuts must instead contract to \(\mathfrak b\). These are precisely the places where the model's support and cotangent hypotheses enter this comparison.

Every prime over \(J+(\mathbf t,a')\) therefore contains \((\mathfrak b\mathscr R)\mathfrak r\), placing this product in the radical of that ideal. It is finitely generated, so radical containment gives the displayed power containment. Explicitly, if generators \(u_i\) satisfy \(u_i^{n_i}\in J+(\mathbf t,a')\), any product of \(1+\sum_i(n_i-1)\) generators contains one of those powers.

2.7.13.9.4 Track the connecting map that makes Euler characteristics additive.

For a graded exact sequence \(0\to N'\to N\to N''\to0\) and a section \(s\) of multidegree \(\delta\), the multiplication diagrams give the exact sequence

\[ \begin{aligned} 0\longrightarrow N'[s](-\delta) &\longrightarrow N[s](-\delta) \longrightarrow N''[s](-\delta)\\ &\longrightarrow N'/sN' \longrightarrow N/sN \longrightarrow N''/sN''\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned} \]

The connecting map is essential. A class killed by \(s\) in \(N''\) can lift to an element of \(N\) whose product by \(s\) is nonzero in \(N'\). The connecting map records that product modulo \(sN'\). Thus quotienting by \(s\) alone need not preserve the original short exact sequence.

Taking alternating lengths makes the connecting contributions cancel. Applying the same argument to the remaining sections proves, by induction,

\[ \chi(\mathbf s;N)_{\mathbf n} =\chi(\mathbf s;N')_{\mathbf n} +\chi(\mathbf s;N'')_{\mathbf n} \qquad(\mathbf n\text{ sufficiently large}). \]

Admissibility supplies the finite lengths needed at the terminal steps of this induction. This is why the subtraction of annihilator terms in §2.7.13.4 is indispensable.

2.7.13.9.5 Check why exchanging two cuts preserves the answer.

For sections \(s,t\) of multidegrees \(\delta,\eta\), their two-step multiplication complex is

\[ 0\longrightarrow N(-\delta-\eta) \xrightarrow{\ z\mapsto(-tz,sz)\ } N(-\delta)\oplus N(-\eta) \xrightarrow{\ (x,y)\mapsto sx+ty\ }N \longrightarrow0. \]

The composite is zero because \(st=ts\). Write its homology modules as \(H_0,H_1,H_2\). Here \(H_0=N/(s,t)N\), \(H_2\) is the common annihilator with the indicated shift, and \(H_1\) records relations between the two cuts modulo the evident relations \((-tz,sz)\).

Expanding the quotient-minus-kernel recursion and using exact-sequence additivity gives, for any remaining list \(\mathbf v\),

\[ \chi((s,t),\mathbf v;N)_{\mathbf n} =\chi(\mathbf v;H_0)_{\mathbf n} -\chi(\mathbf v;H_1)_{\mathbf n} +\chi(\mathbf v;H_2)_{\mathbf n}. \]

Swapping the two middle coordinates and changing the sign on the left identifies this complex with the one for \((t,s)\). All three homology contributions are preserved. Any permutation is a finite sequence of adjacent swaps, so, after choosing one sufficiently large multidegree threshold,

\[ \chi((a'),\mathbf t;\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n} =\chi(\mathbf t,(a');\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n}. \]

The sections need not form a regular sequence. Their failure to be regular is already included in the homology and annihilator terms.

2.7.13.9.6 Prove the nonnegativity used to compare modules.

An alternating sum of lengths is not automatically nonnegative. Here the proof reduces a finitely generated graded module to its homogeneous prime factors. A finite graded prime filtration has factors \((\mathscr R/\mathfrak p)(-\gamma)\). Additivity reduces the question to these factors, and a fixed shift only changes how large \(\mathbf n\) must be.

If \(\mathfrak r\subseteq\mathfrak p\), the factor vanishes in sufficiently large multidegrees. Otherwise put \(D=\mathscr R/\mathfrak p\), a graded domain. Consider the first section \(s\), of degree \(\delta\). If \(s\notin\mathfrak p\), multiplication is injective, and

\[ \chi((\delta,s),\mathbf v;D)_{\mathbf n} =\chi(\mathbf v;D/sD)_{\mathbf n}. \]

If \(s\in\mathfrak p\), it acts as zero. Because \(\mathfrak r\not\subseteq\mathfrak p\) and \(\delta\) is componentwise nonnegative, products of surviving degree-\(e_i\) elements give a section \(\sigma\) of degree \(\delta\) outside \(\mathfrak p\). For degree zero, take \(\sigma=1\). The exact sequence \(0\to D(-\delta)\xrightarrow{\sigma}D\to D/\sigma D\to0\) then gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi((\delta,s),\mathbf v;D)_{\mathbf n} &=\chi(\mathbf v;D)_{\mathbf n} -\chi(\mathbf v;D(-\delta))_{\mathbf n}\\ &=\chi(\mathbf v;D/\sigma D)_{\mathbf n}. \end{aligned} \]

Both cases replace the original expression by one with a shorter section list. Admissibility passes to the resulting quotient. Repeating the argument ends at ordinary finite lengths, which are nonnegative. Summing over the prime filtration proves

\[ \chi(\mathbf s;N)_{\mathbf n}\ge0 \qquad(\mathbf n\text{ sufficiently large}) \]

for the valid, admissible graded data used here. This proof supplies the sign of the corrections below; it does not require their individual modules to vanish.

2.7.13.9.7 Account for the kernel of the leading-term quotient.

Since \(J+(a')\subseteq\mathfrak q\), define

\[ K_{\mathrm{lead}}=\mathfrak q/(J+(a')), \qquad 0\longrightarrow K_{\mathrm{lead}} \longrightarrow\mathcal P/a'\mathcal P \longrightarrow\overline P\longrightarrow0. \]

The full admissibility containment from step 3 becomes admissibility for \(\mathbf t\) on \(\mathcal P/a'\mathcal P\), because \(a'\) now annihilates this module. Its submodule \(K_{\mathrm{lead}}\) inherits the same condition. Additivity and the positivity just proved give

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi(\mathbf t;\mathcal P/a'\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n} -\chi(\mathbf t;\overline P)_{\mathbf n} &=\chi(\mathbf t;K_{\mathrm{lead}})_{\mathbf n}\\ &\ge0. \end{aligned} \]

The inequality follows from the nonnegative Euler contribution of this particular kernel. The existence of the surjection by itself would not establish the sign.

2.7.13.9.8 Keep every correction when the equation cuts come first.

Write \(\mathbf t=(t_1,\ldots,t_k)\), with \(\deg t_j=\delta_j\), and form the successive quotients

\[ \mathcal P_0=\mathcal P,\qquad \mathcal P_j=\mathcal P_{j-1}/t_j\mathcal P_{j-1}, \qquad K_j=\mathcal P_{j-1}[t_j](-\delta_j). \]

The last quotient is \(\mathcal P_k=\mathcal M\). The first recursion subtracts the Euler contribution of \(K_1\); the next recursion subtracts that of \(K_2\) from the surviving quotient branch; continuing gives the exact identity

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi(\mathbf t,(a');\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n} &=\chi((a');\mathcal M)_{\mathbf n}\\ &\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{k} \chi((t_{j+1},\ldots,t_k,a');K_j)_{\mathbf n}. \end{aligned} \]

For \(j=k\), the remaining list is just \((a')\). The earlier equations annihilate \(\mathcal P_{j-1}\), and \(t_j\) annihilates \(K_j\). Thus admissibility of the original list gives admissibility of the remaining list on every \(K_j\). Each summand is eventually nonnegative, and therefore

\[ \chi(\mathbf t,(a');\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n} \le\chi((a');\mathcal M)_{\mathbf n} =\Theta_{a'}(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}). \]

The last equality still subtracts \(\operatorname{length}_B\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}[a']\). It makes no injectivity assertion about the trace on \(\mathcal M\).

2.7.13.9.9 Put the comparison together at one common multidegree.

There are finitely many filtrations, swaps and kernel modules in this argument. Choose \(\mathbf n\) componentwise beyond all their thresholds and the monomial-count threshold from §2.7.13.8. Then the whole comparison holds simultaneously:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2^{u+v}\lambda &\le \chi(\mathbf t;\overline P)_{\mathbf n}\\ &\le \chi(\mathbf t;\mathcal P/a'\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n}\\ &=\chi((a'),\mathbf t;\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n}\\ &=\chi(\mathbf t,(a');\mathcal P)_{\mathbf n}\\ &\le \chi((a');\mathcal M)_{\mathbf n} =\Theta_{a'}(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}). \end{aligned} \]

More explicitly, the entire difference between the two Euler quantities is accounted for:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Theta_{a'}(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}) -\chi(\mathbf t;\overline P)_{\mathbf n} &=\chi(\mathbf t;K_{\mathrm{lead}})_{\mathbf n}\\ &\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{k} \chi((t_{j+1},\ldots,t_k,a');K_j)_{\mathbf n} \ge0. \end{aligned} \]

The lower cotangent count can therefore be carried to the module whose generic components produce trace valuations. The next subsection bounds the multiplicity of those components; this comparison has established the intervening inequality, including every kernel correction.

2.7.13.10 Bound the number of layers at each surviving point.

For a character \(\tau:B\to\overline E\), write \(\mathfrak p_\tau=\ker\tau\). Localizing at this prime isolates the corresponding generic component. Its multiplicity in the graded piece is

\[ m_\tau(\mathbf n)= \operatorname{length}_{B_{\mathfrak p_\tau}} \bigl((\mathcal M_{\mathbf n})_{\mathfrak p_\tau}\bigr). \]

The finite degree-zero quotient and the conditions on one-sided points imply that, in large multidegrees, the surviving generic support comes from two-sided solutions of \(I\). More concretely, it disappears if the degree-zero equations fail or if \(\tau\) kills the ideal generated by all products \(fg\), with \(f\in A_{-2}\) and \(g\in A_2\). This is how the split and one-sided loci, where the trace difference can vanish, leave the generic sum. They are not simply deleted by fiat.

At a surviving point, the transverse slice and cyclic weight-piece argument in §2.7.12.10 gives, for every weight \(w\),

\[ \operatorname{length}_{B_{\mathfrak p_\tau}} \bigl(((A/I)_w)_{\mathfrak p_\tau}\bigr)\le1. \]

Our auxiliary construction used squared equations. Let \(I_{\mathrm{sq}}\) be obtained from \(I\) by replacing each \(F_i,F_*,E_i\) by its square and retaining \(G_j,H_j,Z_j\). This is a selected replacement of generators, not the ideal \(I^2\).

One square can at most double a uniform bound on localized weight-piece lengths. Indeed, for a homogeneous \(t\) of weight \(q\) and a homogeneous ideal \(\mathfrak a\), there is an exact sequence

\[ (A/(\mathfrak a,t))_{w-q} \xrightarrow{\ \cdot t\ }(A/(\mathfrak a,t^2))_w \longrightarrow(A/(\mathfrak a,t))_w \longrightarrow0. \]

The first arrow need not be injective. Its image nevertheless has length at most that of its source, so, after localization,

\[ \ell_\tau(A/(\mathfrak a,t^2))_w \le \ell_\tau(A/(\mathfrak a,t))_{w-q} +\ell_\tau(A/(\mathfrak a,t))_w. \]

Here \(\ell_\tau\) abbreviates length after localization at \(\mathfrak p_\tau\). Starting from the bound \(1\) and repeating for the \(u+1+v\) squared equations gives \(2^{u+1+v}\). The source compares the Rees graded piece with such a weight piece, yielding

\[ m_\tau(\mathbf n) \le \ell_\tau(A/I_{\mathrm{sq}})_{w(\mathbf n)} \le 2^{u+1+v}, \qquad w(\mathbf n)=-2n_0+2n_1-2n_2. \]

This is the upper power of two. Its extra factor comes from the additional equation \(F_*\), whose linear part vanished in the cotangent calculation but whose square still participates in this upper estimate.

2.7.13.11 Sum trace valuations and cancel the common factor.

A prime filtration breaks \(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}\) into contributions from its components. The determinant calculation in §2.7.13.3 applies to each generic component. Finite-length components contribute zero by §2.7.13.4. Additivity therefore gives

\[ \Theta_{a'}(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n}) =\sum_{\tau\in S}m_\tau(\mathbf n)\, v_\varpi\bigl(\tau(a')\bigr). \]

The finite set \(S\) consists of the degree-zero characters covering the surviving generic support, including the relevant embeddings. The source's coefficient-ring hypotheses identify the finite \(B\)-lengths here with \(\mathcal O\)-lengths: their residue fields agree. Thus no residue-degree factor has silently been omitted.

The values \(\tau(a')\) are nonzero and integral, so their valuations are nonnegative. Use the multiplicity bound, then lift the characters to the finite orbit-cover \(\mathcal T\). Enlarging the sum is allowed by nonnegativity, and \(f(a')=f(a)\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} 2^{u+v}\lambda &\le\Theta_{a'}(\mathcal M_{\mathbf n})\\ &\le2^{u+1+v}\sum_{\tau\in S}v_\varpi(\tau(a'))\\ &\le2^{u+1+v}\sum_{f\in\mathcal T}v_\varpi(f(a)). \end{aligned} \]

Divide by the positive number \(2^{u+v}\). Then divide by the ramification index \(e_{\mathcal O}\), using \(v_\varpi=e_{\mathcal O}v_\ell\):

\[ \boxed{\quad \frac{\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{-1}}{e_{\mathcal O}} \le2\sum_{f\in\mathcal T} v_\ell\!\left(\operatorname{tr}\rho_f(\sigma)-t_0(\sigma)\right). \quad} \]

The representation-theoretic application chooses distinct orbit representatives and identifies their representations up to isomorphism. Combining this with \(L_\ell(\psi)\le\lambda/e_{\mathcal O}\) from §2.7.11 yields

\[ L_\ell(\psi)\le 2\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal X_\psi} v_\ell\!\left(\operatorname{tr}\rho(\sigma)-t_0(\sigma)\right). \]

Distinct representation classes with the same numerical trace still contribute separately. The next subsection packages these nonzero differences into an integer whose total prime divisibility is bounded.

A small model: rigid generic orbits can still leave integral torsion.

This illustration explains the phenomenon; it does not prove the graded comparison or its factor of two. Let \(c=\varpi^n\), \(n\ge1\), and set

\[ R_{\mathrm{toy}}= \mathcal O[u_0,v_0]/ \bigl(u_0(u_0v_0-c),\,v_0(u_0v_0-c)\bigr), \qquad \deg u_0=-1,\quad\deg v_0=1. \]

At the origin, the linearized equations are \(-cu_0\) and \(-cv_0\). The negative cotangent direction therefore has quotient \(\mathcal O/(c)\), of length \(n\). Over the fraction field, a solution is either the origin or satisfies \(u_0v_0=c\). The latter points form one grading orbit, with no transverse deformation. The invariant \(a_{\mathrm{toy}}=u_0v_0\) takes the value \(c\) there, so

\[ \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O}C_{\mathrm{toy},-1} =n=v_\varpi(c). \]

The invariant is not an actual Galois trace. It shows how the distance of a rigid orbit from the base value can remember integral torsion that disappears after inverting \(\varpi\).

2.7.14 Turn trace differences into one nonzero integer.

The estimate still involves \(\ell\)-adic representations. To sum over many coefficient primes, the source uses the compatible automorphic trace data. Choose an auxiliary rational prime \(p\) away from \(30N|\operatorname{disc}K|\), a place \(w\) over it, and write \(q\ge7\) for the residue-field size. The source evaluates at \(\sigma=\operatorname{Frob}_w^{-1}\). For its chosen identification \(\iota:\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\simeq\mathbb C\), put \(B=q^r\) and \(z_\psi=\iota(\psi(q))^{-1}\). Then the complex realization of the reference trace is

\[ t_0=B^{-1}z_\psi+1+Bz_\psi^{-1}. \]

The automorphic input supplies a finite conjugation-stable family of algebraic trace labels \(t_s\), with \(|t_s|\le3\) at every complex embedding and \(Bt_s\) integral. Their \(\ell\)-adic realizations cover the representations needed in the preceding estimate. For a conjugation-stable character orbit \(\mathscr O\), the product is

\[ N_p=\prod_{\psi\in\mathscr O}\prod_s B\bigl(t_s-B^{-1}z_\psi-1-Bz_\psi^{-1}\bigr)\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}. \]

The calculation in §2.6.6.1 proves integrality, conjugation invariance, nonvanishing and the height bound. Its key nonvanishing observation is that \(B^2z_\psi^{-1}\) is too large in complex absolute value to be canceled by the other terms: \(B^2>4B+1\). This supplies the trace separation required in §2.7.13.

For \(\ell\ne p\), the denominator-clearing factor \(B\) is an \(\ell\)-adic unit. In the valuation formula below the algebraic trace labels and character values are transported back through \(\iota^{-1}\); we keep the same symbols for those \(\ell\)-adic realizations. All the resulting factors are integral, so their valuations are nonnegative. We can enlarge the finite representation lists to the common trace family without decreasing the bound, then use additivity of valuation on the product:

\[ \sum_{\psi\in\mathscr O}L_\ell(\psi) \le2\sum_{\psi\in\mathscr O}\sum_s v_\ell\bigl(B(t_s-t_{0,\psi})\bigr) =2v_\ell(N_p). \]

This explains both sides of the construction. Complex absolute values ensure that the integer is nonzero and bound its size. Its \(\ell\)-adic valuations pay for the possible depth of the integral deformations.

2.7.15 Sum the local bounds and return to the arithmetic window.

A nonzero integer has only a finite amount of prime divisibility. For any finite set \(P\) of qualifying coefficient primes,

\[ \sum_{\ell\in P}v_\ell(N_p)\log\ell\le\log|N_p|. \]

Choose a second auxiliary prime \(p'\ne p\). The integer built using \(p'\) also covers the coefficient prime \(\ell=p\), where the first argument could not discard its denominator. Using both nonnegative budgets gives

\[ \sum_{\ell\in P}\log\ell \sum_{\psi\in\mathscr O_\ell}L_\ell(\psi) \le2\log|N_p|+2\log|N_{p'}|. \]

The subscript on \(\mathscr O_\ell\) allows the coefficient realization of the same finite character orbit to vary with \(\ell\). The two integers and their size bounds are chosen before that coefficient prime. This uniformity is what makes the sum useful.

More precisely, the source obtains constants \(c_5,A\), depending on the fixed auxiliary field, such that for a character orbit associated to a subgroup \(H\subseteq(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times\),

\[ \log|N_p|\le c_5\,\varphi([ (\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times:H ]) \bigl(N(r+1)\bigr)^A\log p. \]

Here \(\varphi\) is Euler's totient function. Counting the trace labels and controlling their heights therefore turn the cohomological sum into a polynomial arithmetic budget, independently of how many qualifying coefficient primes were initially put into \(P\).

A shared local language

2.8 Make both sides use the same local parameter.

2.8.1 Put the local automorphic data into a comparable form.

A representation of \(\operatorname{GL}_n(E_v)\) is a different kind of object from a representation of the local Galois group. The local correspondence supplies a map into Weil–Deligne parameters:

\[ \operatorname{rc}_v: \{\text{irreducible local }\operatorname{GL}_n\text{ representations, all }n\} \longrightarrow \{\text{Weil–Deligne parameters}\}, \qquad \dim\operatorname{rc}_v(\sigma)=n. \]

Now both sides of the Galois comparison live in the same mathematical language. A Weil parameter describes local arithmetic symmetries; its monodromy operator retains extra ramification information that eigenvalues alone can miss.

2.8.2 Anchor the correspondence in one-dimensional reciprocity.

In rank one, a smooth character \(\chi:E_v^\times\to\mathbb C^\times\) becomes a Weil-group character by composition with the local Artin map:

\[ \operatorname{Art}_v:W_{E_v}\longrightarrow E_v^\times, \qquad \operatorname{rc}_v(\chi)=\chi\circ\operatorname{Art}_v. \]

This fixes the rank-one normalization. In higher rank, the source first supplies parameters on cuspidal representations, then extends them using Langlands multisegments. Its laws preserve dimension, describe this extension, send cuspidal representations to irreducible parameters, and respect real norm twists.

2.8.3 Preserve the hypotheses on the stronger laws.

Several further laws require the source’s condition \(\mathrm{Good}\). It asks that the cuspidal support consist of rank-one pieces or specified character twists of representations having nonzero \(K_1\)-fixed vectors, with a local-field condition. Twisting, exact central-character compatibility, duality, injectivity, and the stated depth implication retain this restriction.

For example, the injectivity clause reads

\[ \mathrm{Good}(\sigma_2),\quad \operatorname{rc}_v(\sigma_1)=\operatorname{rc}_v(\sigma_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_1=\sigma_2. \]

Removing the first hypothesis would assert more than the inspected theorem. The same discipline applies when these laws are used to compare Galois and automorphic data.

2.8.4 Construct the local parameter, then check exactly which laws it satisfies.

The local map is built in stages: characters first, selected cuspidal representations next, then segments and multisegments. This is more informative than simply asserting that an automorphic representation has a Weil–Deligne parameter. It explains where the dimension, twists, monodromy and restrictions on the comparison come from.

2.8.4.1 Keep both parts of a Weil–Deligne parameter.

Fix the local field \(k=E_v\). A Weil–Deligne parameter consists of a Weil-group representation \(R\) and a nilpotent operator \(N\), with the prescribed compatibility between conjugation by \(R(w)\) and scaling of \(N\). The map being constructed is

\[ \operatorname{rc}:\operatorname{Irr}(\mathrm{GL}_n(k)) \longrightarrow\{(R,N)\text{ of dimension }n\}/\simeq. \]

Here irreducible representations are taken up to isomorphism, and the construction handles all ranks together. In rank one, the chosen local Artin map gives

\[ \operatorname{rc}(\chi) =(\chi\circ\operatorname{Art}_v,0). \]

The orientation of the Artin map fixes the Frobenius convention. We keep that convention throughout. Write \(\nu\) for the real norm twist on the automorphic side and \(\nu_W\) for its matching Weil-group twist; then \(\operatorname{rc}(\sigma\nu^s)=\operatorname{rc}(\sigma)\nu_W^s\) is the normalization used below.

2.8.4.2 Build the controlled cuspidal parameters from finite-field characters.

The source's controlled higher-rank cuspidal case requires \(k\simeq\mathbb Q_p\) and a representation with nonzero fixed vectors for the specified subgroup \(K_1\). Its local type is described by a cuspidal representation of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb F_p)\). The finite-field character theorem associates to this type a regular character

\[ \theta:\mathbb F_{p^n}^{\times}\to\mathbb C^{\times}, \qquad \theta,\theta^p,\ldots,\theta^{p^{n-1}} \text{ are distinct}. \]

Regularity means that the Frobenius orbit has length \(n\). The character is determined only up to that orbit, which is exactly the ambiguity that the resulting parameter should ignore.

A tame residue map \(R_I:I_k\to\mathbb F_{p^n}^{\times}\) then gives the inertia characters

\[ \eta_j(t)=\theta\bigl(R_I(t)^{p^j}\bigr), \qquad 0\le j<n, \qquad \det(T-R(t))=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(T-\eta_j(t)). \]

Frobenius permutes these characters cyclically. This constructs an \(n\)-dimensional Weil representation: inertia acts diagonally, and Frobenius moves between the corresponding lines. Distinctness of the inertia characters and transitivity of the permutation force irreducibility. A stable subspace must be a sum of inertia eigenspaces; including any one of them forces inclusion of the entire Frobenius orbit.

The remaining scalar in the Frobenius cycle is chosen to match the central character \(\omega_\sigma\). For a cyclic matrix \(F\) with wraparound scalar \(\lambda\),

\[ F^n=\lambda I,\qquad \det F=(-1)^{n-1}\lambda, \qquad \det R(w)=\omega_\sigma(\operatorname{Art}_v(w)). \]

The inertia determinant is checked using the norm from \(\mathbb F_{p^n}\) to \(\mathbb F_p\). Wild inertia acts trivially in this construction, and the irreducible parameter has \(N=0\). These are conclusions of the controlled cuspidal construction, not assertions about every local representation.

2.8.4.3 Extend across character twists without depending on a presentation.

A higher-rank \(\mathrm{GoodCusp}\) representation can be a smooth character twist of the preceding case. If \(\sigma=\sigma_0\otimes(\chi\circ\det)\), set

\[ R_\sigma=R_{\sigma_0}\otimes(\chi\circ\operatorname{Art}_v). \]

A representation might admit two such presentations. The definition is useful only if they give isomorphic parameters. The source proves this using uniqueness of the underlying tame parameter and compatibility with twists. Concretely,

\[ \sigma_0\otimes\chi\simeq\sigma_1\otimes\chi_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{\sigma_0}\otimes(\chi\circ\operatorname{Art}_v) \simeq R_{\sigma_1}\otimes(\chi_1\circ\operatorname{Art}_v). \]

In the intermediate comparison, the ratio of the two twisting characters is tame when both untwisted representations have the specified \(K_1\)-fixed vectors. This lets the tame uniqueness theorem apply. Rank-one characters are checked separately against the Artin normalization. The result is a well-defined cuspidal parameter on the controlled class.

2.8.4.4 Understand why the map outside the controlled class has weaker guarantees.

The actual existence proof defines a map on every cuspidal isomorphism class. On \(\mathrm{GoodCusp}\) it uses the preceding construction. Outside that class it chooses auxiliary irreducible parameters of the right dimension and adjusts their real norm twist to match the absolute central-character condition. Schematically, its case distinction is

\[ R_\sigma= \begin{cases} R^{\mathrm{good}}_\sigma,&\mathrm{GoodCusp}(\sigma),\\ J_n\otimes\nu_W^{s(\sigma)},&\text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad\sigma\text{ is a representation of }\mathrm{GL}_n(k). \]

Here \(n\) denotes the rank of the general linear group, not the vector-space dimension of its usually infinite-dimensional smooth representation. The auxiliary choice \(J_n\) is accompanied by the separation properties required in the proof. It supplies the weaker laws demanded outside the controlled class; it is not an identification of those arbitrary cuspidal representations with their usual full local Langlands parameters.

This explains the placement of the hypotheses in §2.8.3. Dimension, cuspidal irreducibility and real norm twisting hold for the whole constructed map. Exact central characters, arbitrary character twisting, duality, and the stronger uniqueness statements retain their \(\mathrm{Good}\) assumptions. A total map with restricted laws must be read with those restrictions.

2.8.4.5 Turn one segment into a parameter with a monodromy chain.

A segment begins with a cuspidal representation \(\sigma\) of \(\mathrm{GL}_d(k)\) and has length \(m\ge1\):

\[ \Delta=(\sigma,\sigma\nu,\ldots,\sigma\nu^{m-1}). \]

It contributes rank \(dm\). The source assigns the parameter

\[ S(\Delta)= \bigl(R_\sigma\otimes\nu_W^{(m-1)/2}\bigr) \otimes\operatorname{Sp}_m. \]

The special parameter \(\operatorname{Sp}_m\) has dimension \(m\), symmetrically normalized norm weights, and a single nilpotent chain of length \(m\). The factor \(\nu_W^{(m-1)/2}\) changes the symmetric normalization to the segment's exponents \(0,1,\ldots,m-1\). On the underlying Weil representation, its characteristic polynomial is therefore

\[ \det(T-S(\Delta)(w)) =\prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \det\bigl(T-(R_\sigma\otimes\nu_W^j)(w)\bigr). \]

The tensor-product monodromy is \(N_{R_\sigma}\otimes1+1\otimes N_{\operatorname{Sp}_m}\). Since the cuspidal parameter has zero monodromy, this becomes \(1\otimes N_{\operatorname{Sp}_m}\). The segment length is consequently retained as a nilpotent chain, rather than disappearing when the Weil eigenvalues are listed.

2.8.4.6 See why eigenvalues alone lose information.

Take a rank-one character \(\chi\) and the length-two segment \((\chi,\chi\nu)\). Put \(\xi=\chi\circ\operatorname{Art}_v\). Its two-dimensional parameter has

\[ P_w(T)=(T-\xi(w))(T-\xi(w)\nu_W(w)), \qquad N\sim\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The direct sum of the two length-one parameters has the same underlying Weil characteristic polynomial, but has \(N=0\). These are different Weil–Deligne parameters. In the segment parameter the two lines are linked by monodromy; in the direct sum they are not. Thus a proof comparing only characteristic polynomials cannot automatically claim equality of full local parameters. This is the concrete reason for the monodromy restrictions in §2.7.5.

2.8.4.7 Assemble all segments and prove independence of choices.

The Langlands classification supplies a multisegment \(\mathfrak m=\{\Delta_1,\ldots,\Delta_r\}\) for an irreducible representation \(\pi_v\). Its segments determine essentially square-integrable pieces; the appropriately ordered normalized induction has \(\pi_v\) as its Langlands quotient. The parameter is defined by

\[ \operatorname{rc}(\pi_v)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}S(\Delta_i). \]

The classification and uniqueness of the extension ensure that this depends on the isomorphism class of \(\pi_v\), rather than on a chosen ordering or presentation. The basic checks are now visible:

\[ \dim\operatorname{rc}(\pi_v) =\sum_i d_i m_i=n, \qquad P_{\operatorname{rc}(\pi_v)(w)}(T) =\prod_i P_{S(\Delta_i)(w)}(T). \]

A cuspidal representation corresponds to a single segment of length one, so the extension agrees with the original cuspidal map. Real norm twists shift every segment together. Direct sums preserve the determinant product, while the segment formula keeps track of the nilpotent blocks. These observations explain the dimension, cuspidal and multisegment laws filled by the existence theorem.

2.8.4.8 Carry the controlled laws from cuspidal support to the whole representation.

Assume \(\mathrm{Good}(\pi_v)\): every cuspidal constituent in its support lies in the controlled class. Then each segment can use the exact cuspidal twisting and determinant formulas. For example, determinants multiply across the support, yielding

\[ \det\operatorname{rc}(\pi_v)(w) =\prod_{\sigma\text{ in the cuspidal support}} \det R_\sigma(w) =\omega_{\pi_v}(\operatorname{Art}_v(w)). \]

The product retains multiplicities and the segment twists. Similarly, dualizing the segment data and their monodromy chains proves the duality law. Uniqueness of the parameter decomposition, together with the controlled cuspidal uniqueness statement, gives the restricted injectivity conclusion:

\[ \mathrm{Good}(\pi_2),\quad \operatorname{rc}(\pi_1)=\operatorname{rc}(\pi_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_1=\pi_2. \]

The source proves these extension results before assembling the final record of laws. Merely writing the record would not establish that any map satisfies it. Here the construction of the cuspidal map, the multisegment existence theorem and the separate law proofs supply the witnesses.

2.8.4.9 Use the constructed map at exactly the strength the arithmetic needs.

The Galois representation theorem is formulated for a local map satisfying these particular laws. The existence theorem supplies such a map, so the two results can be composed:

\[ \begin{gathered} \exists\operatorname{rc},\ \operatorname{Laws}(\operatorname{rc}),\\ \forall\operatorname{rc},\ \operatorname{Laws}(\operatorname{rc}) \Longrightarrow\operatorname{Comparison}(r,\pi,\operatorname{rc})\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \text{the comparison for the constructed }\operatorname{rc}. \end{gathered} \]

The content lies in the meaning of comparison. The local hypotheses in §2.7.5 remain in force: the toral case and zero-monodromy assumption, or the specified tame and squarefree conditions with the local-field and rank bound, give their respective conclusions. The stronger laws retain \(\mathrm{Good}\). Neither the existence of a total map nor its dimension law upgrades these statements to unrestricted local–global compatibility.

The theta and representation branch

2.9 Make two descriptions carry the same information.

The theta branch connects local representation data to global integrals, test vectors, and evaluation conditions. Its role is to make the arithmetic and geometric estimates apply to one compatible construction. The steps below first assemble that construction, then explain its common lattice, correlation bounds, slope comparison, and main-term cancellation.

2.9.1 Form an integral, then justify that it converges.

A test function \(\Phi\) is an input that we can vary. The Weil representation \(\omega(g,h)\) transforms it using elements of two unitary groups. The model’s theta sum turns the transformed function into \(\theta_\Phi(g,h)\). Integrating in the first variable leaves a function of the second:

\[ \theta_\Phi(g,h)=\Theta\bigl(\omega(g,h)\Phi\bigr), \qquad I(\Phi,h)=\int_{\mathcal D}\theta_\Phi(g,h)\,d\mu(g). \]

Here \(\Theta\), \(\mathcal D\), and \(\mu\) abbreviate the model’s theta sum, integration domain, and measure. The point is that a local-to-global construction has produced something on which analytic operations can act. An integral symbol alone does not justify those operations: the source separately requires integrability for every allowed \(h\), and supplies decay and moderate-growth conditions for the specified test spaces. These conditions control the behavior at infinity.

2.9.2 Match the local images of the test functions.

The two models use different test spaces. Matching does not mean that their functions are equal. Instead, local maps send both spaces to common representation spaces. At infinity call the maps \(p_\infty,p'_\infty\); at a finite place \(v\), call them \(p_v,p'_v\). For a single tensor, the matching condition has the schematic form

\[ p_\infty(\phi_\infty)\otimes\bigotimes_{v\in T}p_v(\phi_v) = p'_\infty(\phi'_\infty)\otimes\bigotimes_{v\in T}p'_v(\phi'_v). \]

The finite set \(T\) includes the exceptional places; outside it, the construction uses prescribed vacuum vectors. The actual definition allows finite sums of tensors, then extends matching through specified limiting sequences. This retains the shared representation-theoretic information while allowing different formulas on the two sides. The local matching theorem supplies the required maps under the admissibility hypotheses.

2.9.3 Transfer information through a nonzero scalar.

For fixed admissible models and matching maps, the global comparison supplies a unique constant \(c\ne0\) such that every matched pair satisfies

\[ I(\Phi,h)=c\,I'(\Phi',h),\qquad I'(\Phi',h)=c^{-1}I(\Phi,h). \]

The same constant works throughout this comparison; it is not chosen anew at each \(h\). Consequently, for matched data,

\[ I(\Phi,h)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ I'(\Phi',h)=0, \qquad |I(\Phi,h)|=|c|\,|I'(\Phi',h)|. \]

The second identity also shows why normalizations matter: comparison does not make \(|c|=1\). The source additionally supplies a nonzero integral on the second side and identities relating successive lift stages. It does not assert that every test function gives a nonzero integral. These are comparison inputs, not yet the desired nonvanishing theorem for Dirichlet L-functions.

2.9.4 Choose test vectors that detect the required information.

An identity between families of integrals becomes useful only after choosing inputs that detect the features we need. The source combines the comparison with separation results for functionals, constructs rational test-vector data, and proves a Fourier-coefficient fitting condition. Rationality is what allows these analytic constructions to interact with arithmetic structures.

The elementary linear-algebra picture of separation is helpful. A family of linear measurements \(\lambda_i\) separates vectors when

\[ \bigcap_i\ker\lambda_i=\{0\}. \]

Then agreement on every measurement forces agreement of the vectors: apply the equation to their difference. This is the intuition for the detection step, not a replacement for the source’s functional and Fourier-fitting hypotheses. Its actual construction also needs local matching, compatible characters, and the specified automorphic inputs to obtain usable test vectors and an evaluation grid.

2.9.5 Put the grid and the functional bounds at one scale.

Two construction theorems supply eventual thresholds. Write \(C_{\mathrm{grid}}\) for the threshold of the evaluation-grid theorem and \(C_{\mathrm{fun}}\) for the threshold of the functional theorem. Once the other compatibility hypotheses hold, each conclusion is available for every sufficiently large parameter. Set

\[ C_*=\max\{C_{\mathrm{grid}},C_{\mathrm{fun}}\}. \]

Both comparisons now apply:

\[ C_*\ge C_{\mathrm{grid}},\qquad C_*\ge C_{\mathrm{fun}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{both conclusions hold at }C_*. \]

This is an actual step in the assembly. It works because the statements hold beyond their thresholds. Two unrelated existence assertions, each at one unspecified parameter, would not justify taking a maximum.

2.9.6 Carry the geometry into that same construction.

The proof fixes the local correspondence, uses the grid at \(C_*\), and passes to an extended parameter system retaining the required compatibility conditions. A transport theorem supplies the covolume estimate for these same parameters. The Galois estimate and functional condition are assembled there too.

Logically, the required output has the form

\[ \exists D,\quad \mathcal A(D)\ \land\ \mathcal F(D)\ \land\ \mathcal G(D), \]

where \(\mathcal A,\mathcal F,\mathcal G\) schematically denote the arithmetic, functional, and geometric conditions. Separate witnesses for each condition would not suffice. The resulting compatible family feeds the aggregate prime-sum estimate, together with the Green input; the arithmetic window then supplies the character-specific data used by Landau.

2.9.7 Compare the periods inside one common space.

Why is matching needed for a quantitative estimate? A norm bound for each period separately does not tell us whether different periods detect different directions. We need to compare their images. The seesaw construction supplies that common setting: it moves a lift from one side of a pairing to the other, with the test data and normalizations carried along.

For a choice of character data \(i\), the period comparison has the form

\[ B_i(\phi,F)=a_i\,B_i^{\mathrm{Bes}}(U_i\phi,VF), \qquad a_i\ne0. \]

Here \(U_i\) is an isometry of the source and \(V\) is one fixed isometry of the target, shared by every \(i\). This last condition lets us take inner products between the images of different character choices. The scalar \(a_i\) remains part of the calculation: its absolute values and finite-place valuations affect the height and divisibility estimates.

The arithmetic construction also provides lattices \(N_i\), integral period maps \(B_i:N_i\to C\), and height allowances \(h_i\). The common lattice \(C\) is in a dual period space. Local divisibility permits the prescribed adjustment of each \(N_i\), while the resulting maps still land in \(C\). Thus the same maps have both an integral meaning at finite places and a Hermitian norm at infinity. That compatibility is what allows the next linear-algebra estimate to constrain arithmetic size.

2.9.8 Derive the correlations and measure coverage of the target.

The period maps must do two jobs: preserve enough of each source and distribute those sources across the common target. The second requirement is essential: a collection of isometries could otherwise have exactly the same image. Here is the chain from the orbital calculation to the two quantitative tests used by the slope argument.

2.9.8.1 Fix the operators and the comparison scale.

At a fixed infinite place, write the full period matrices as \(\widetilde B_i:V_i\to H\). Their prescribed retained target coordinates give the integral maps \(B_i\) of the preceding step. In orthonormal coordinates set \(A_i=\sqrt{c_i}\,\widetilde B_i\), with the positive normalization \(c_i\) coming from the period identity. Put

\[ r_i=\dim V_i,\qquad R=\sum_i r_i>0,\qquad D=\dim H>0,\qquad K_{ij}=A_i^*A_j,\qquad Q=\sum_iA_iA_i^*. \]

The Hilbert–Schmidt norm satisfies \(\|M\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\operatorname{tr}(M^*M)\). Thus \(K_{ii}-I\) measures distortion in one source, while \(K_{ij}\), for \(i\ne j\), measures overlap between two sources. The natural cross-term scale is \(r_ir_j/D_*\), where \(D_*>0\) is the dimension predicted by the mass and volume calculation. The actual target dimension \(D\) is compared with \(D_*\) separately; these are not interchangeable definitions.

The argument applies to the compatible family already constructed: its archimedean weights are separated, its local models and measures agree, and its level parameters satisfy the required congruences. The auxiliary rank and level must also exceed the thresholds below. The pair estimate is not a claim about arbitrary automorphic forms.

2.9.8.2 Unfold the overlap and remove degenerate off-diagonal terms.

Unfolding expands \(K_{ij}\) into orbital terms. Absolute convergence permits regrouping them into the identity contribution, a degenerate contribution \(K^{\mathrm{deg}}_{ij}\), and the remaining terms \(K^0_{ij}\). The identity contribution is \(I\) when \(i=j\) and vanishes for distinct words. This is why the diagonal is compared with the identity rather than with zero.

The degenerate contribution disappears off the diagonal for a more specific reason. Its orbital operator intertwines the two archimedean representations on the subgroup fixing the relevant flag subspaces. The separated highest weights imply that this intertwiner is zero. Terms outside the relevant orbits vanish as well. Consequently, for \(i\ne j\),

\[ K^{\mathrm{deg}}_{ij}=0,\qquad K_{ij}=K^0_{ij}. \]

The surviving squared norm is organized into rows of pairs of orbital terms. Different rows with the same finite index are orthogonal. Within a row, pairs divide according to a corank statistic: corank at least two, corank one with nonscalar generic data, corank one with scalar generic data, and nongeneric data. The scalar generic class carries the main term. Bounds for the other classes must be small after normalization, rather than merely finite.

2.9.8.3 Control the finite Gram matrix with a factor approaching one.

At the selected finite places, the row calculation contains a Gram matrix built from a Whittaker character of the upper unipotent subgroup \(U\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_k(\mathbf F_q)\). Averaging that character over conjugates gives a central convolution operator. On a nongeneric irreducible representation it acts by zero; on a generic one its normalized eigenvalue is \(|U|/\dim\pi\). Multiplicity one and the mirabolic dimension estimate give

\[ |U|=q^{k(k-1)/2},\qquad \dim\pi\ge\prod_{a=1}^{k-1}(q^a-1), \qquad \frac{|U|}{\dim\pi} \le\prod_{a=1}^{k-1}(1-q^{-a})^{-1} \le1+\frac3q\quad(q\ge3). \]

This bounds the Gram operator by a scalar multiple of the identity. Tensoring over the selected places multiplies the scalar losses. If \(g_1,g_2\) are the two selected rational prime sizes and \(f\) is the number of real places, the resulting price \(P_g\) is bounded by

\[ 0\le P_g\le(1+3/g_1)^f(1+3/g_2)^f \le \exp(6b^{-7}) \]

provided \(b\ge1\), \(1\le k\), \(f\le k\), and \(g_1,g_2\ge(kb)^8\). Here \(b\) is the growing size parameter of the family. The large auxiliary primes make the finite Gram operator almost contractive. Keeping this factor near one is necessary for covering almost the whole target.

2.9.8.4 Count the main rows and bound the other rows.

The main scalar rows are evaluated through the mass formula, which separates an archimedean volume from a product of local representation densities. Those densities count frames with prescribed pairings. For example, over \(\mathbf F_q\), let \(\phi:V\to U_0\) be surjective and let \(z_1,\ldots,z_\ell\) be independent. Set \(d_0=\dim\ker\phi\) and \(s=\dim\langle\phi(z_1),\ldots,\phi(z_\ell)\rangle\). The exact count for the next independent vector is

\[ \#\{x:\phi(x)=a,\ x\notin\langle z_1,\ldots,z_\ell\rangle\} = \begin{cases} q^{d_0}-q^{\ell-s},& a\in\langle\phi(z_1),\ldots,\phi(z_\ell)\rangle,\\ q^{d_0},&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Indeed, every nonempty fiber is a translate of \(\ker\phi\), giving \(q^{d_0}\) choices. If that fiber meets the already chosen span, its intersection has \(q^{\ell-s}\) elements, which must be removed. Iterating this count gives the split local factors. For inert places the analogous induction peels off a vector of nonzero norm, reducing the ambient dimension by one, or an isotropic vector and its paired direction, reducing it by two. These exact recursions are the source of the local-density error estimates.

The global mass identity and the compatible archimedean normalization then put the scalar generic row sum on the scale \(d_{ij}=r_ir_j/D_*\). The remaining archimedean and lattice-counting bounds control the departure from that scale. Writing \(A_{\mathrm{main}}\) for the normalized scalar-row upper bound, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{loop}}\) for each of the large-corank and nonscalar errors, and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{ng}}\) for the nongeneric error, the row estimates combine as

\[ \|K^0_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \le \left( \sqrt{P_g(A_{\mathrm{main}}+2\epsilon_{\mathrm{loop}})} +\sqrt{P_g(\epsilon_{\mathrm{loop}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ng}})} \right)^2 d_{ij}. \]

The two square roots come from bounding the generic and nongeneric vectors separately and then applying the triangle inequality. Within the generic part there are two error classes, explaining the coefficient \(2\). The scalar main row is a signed quadratic sum; the argument does not replace every individual contribution by its absolute value and thereby lose the leading constant.

The estimates have quantitative room. The loop error has the form \(\exp(-kf\log k/3)\). The nongeneric bound contains a decaying factor \(2^{-(k-2r_s-5)}\) multiplied by \(\exp(Cm_0^5f\log b)\), where \(r_s\) is the smaller flag rank and \(m_0=2r_s+2\). Taking \(k\) large enough to dominate \(Cm_0^5f\log b\) makes the first factor win. For example, the numerical steps use \(k\ge4r_s+10\), \(k\ge8Cm_0^5f\log b\), and \(k\ge70\log b\) to reach a bound \(b^{-14}\). The density error has the explicit reduction

\[ 12f\,2^{-(k-2r_s-2)} \le f\,2^{-k/2} \le e^{-k/5} \le b^{-14}, \qquad f\le k,\quad k\ge\max(32,4r_s+12,70\log b). \]

Thus the surplus ambient dimension controls the product of the local-density corrections, even while the number of places grows.

After the family thresholds are imposed, the precise numerical targets are

\[ A_{\mathrm{main}}\le(1+b^{-7})e^{b^{-14}}, \qquad \epsilon_{\mathrm{loop}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{ng}}\le b^{-14}. \]

For \(b\ge16\), substituting these and the Gram price into the preceding square-root expression makes it at most \(1+b^{-6}\). Together with the vanishing of the degenerate off-diagonal terms, this gives the desired sharp comparison

\[ \boxed{\ \|K_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \le(1+\eta_0)\frac{r_ir_j}{D_*}, \qquad \eta_0=b^{-6},\quad i\ne j.\ } \]

The finite-field count alone does not prove this global bound: the mass identity, convergent unfolding, archimedean orbital estimates, and the selected size conditions are all used in the passage to the displayed row bounds.

2.9.8.5 Recover the diagonal error and compare the two target dimensions.

On the diagonal, the degenerate part need not vanish. The source bounds it by \(r_i e^{-kf\log k}\), while the remainder after subtracting the identity has squared norm at most \(\Gamma r_i^2/D_*\). Here \(\Gamma>1\) is the common normalization parameter. The admissible rank window supplies \(r_i\Gamma^2/D_*\le C_0\), with fixed \(C_0\ge1\). Therefore

\[ \|K_{ii}-I\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \le 2\Gamma\frac{r_i^2}{D_*} +2r_i e^{-kf\log k} \le e_0r_i,\qquad e_0=\frac{2C_0}{\Gamma}+2e^{-kf\log k}. \]

The factor \(2\) comes from \(\|X+Y\|^2\le2\|X\|^2+2\|Y\|^2\). This explains both pieces of the distortion error: the first is reduced by the common normalization and rank window; the second is the residual degenerate contribution. Summing over \(i\) gives \(\sum_i\|K_{ii}-I\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\le e_0R\).

A separate counting estimate compares the actual target with its mass prediction:

\[ |D-D_*|\le\eta_1D_*, \qquad \eta_1=e^{-c_7rf\log r}, \qquad c_7>0. \]

Here \(r\) is the ambient rank in that counting problem. For the selected family, \(\log b=x^\varepsilon\), and \(\log\Gamma=C_\Gamma m^5f\log b\) with fixed \(C_\Gamma>0\). The choices enforce \(k\ge8\log\Gamma\), \(r\ge k\), \(f\ge1\), and \(k\ge5\). To see the resulting rate, put \(y=x^{\varepsilon/8}\), so \(\log b=y^8\). For sufficiently large \(y\),

\[ e_0\le(2C_0+2)e^{-C_\Gamma y^8}\le e^{-2y}, \qquad 8c_7C_\Gamma y^8\ge y. \]

The first inequality uses \(kf\log k\ge\log\Gamma\); the second controls the target-dimension error because \(rf\log r\ge k\ge8C_\Gamma y^8\). Since \(b^{-6}=e^{-6y^8}\), all three losses then satisfy, uniformly throughout the chosen family,

\[ \eta_0,\ \eta_1,\ \sqrt{e_0} \le e^{-x^{\varepsilon/8}}. \]

Thus the correlation parameters are supplied by specific counts and norm estimates. They are not arbitrary small constants assumed at the end.

2.9.8.6 Sum the pair estimates, including the diagonal price.

The identities relating the source overlaps to the target operator are

\[ T:=\operatorname{tr}Q=\sum_i\operatorname{tr}K_{ii}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(Q^2)=\sum_{i,j}\|K_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2. \]

Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(|T-R|\le\sqrt{R}\sqrt{\sum_i\|K_{ii}-I\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2}\le R\sqrt{e_0}\). Also \(\|K_{ii}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\le(1+\sqrt{e_0})\sqrt{r_i}\). Summing the diagonal and off-diagonal terms separately therefore yields

\[ T\ge(1-\sqrt{e_0})R,\qquad \operatorname{tr}(Q^2) \le(1+\eta_0)\frac{R^2}{D_*} +(1+\sqrt{e_0})^2R. \]

For \(e_0<1\), divide by \(T^2\) and use \(D\le(1+\eta_1)D_*\):

\[ \frac{D\,\operatorname{tr}(Q^2)}{T^2} \le \frac{(1+\eta_1) \left(1+\eta_0+(1+\sqrt{e_0})^2D_*/R\right)} {(1-\sqrt{e_0})^2}. \]

This exposes an additional requirement: \(R\) must greatly exceed \(D_*\). Even perfect off-diagonal estimates leave a diagonal price of order \(D_*/R\). The many members of the family provide this surplus. More precisely, if \(0<\sigma\le1/4\), \(R\ge D_*/\sigma\), and \(\eta_0,\eta_1,\sqrt{e_0}\le\sigma/2\), the source bounds the last expression by \(1+5\sigma\). One may therefore take \(e=e_0\) and \(\eta=5\sigma\) in the frame inequalities.

Some source columns must then be restricted to the prescribed ramified subspaces. These restrictions use isometric inclusions, so they compress each \(K_{ii}\) and cannot enlarge its Hilbert–Schmidt defect. If the removed columns have total dimension at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{col}}R\), the trace lost is at most

\[ T_{\mathrm{full}}-T_{\mathrm{restricted}} \le\left(\delta_{\mathrm{col}} +\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{col}}e_0}\right)R. \]

The positive operator decreases under this restriction. Combining that fact with the trace bound gives the usable parameters \(e=2e_0\) and \(\eta=5\sigma+3\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{col}}}\), under \(0<\sigma\le1/20\), \(0\le\delta_{\mathrm{col}}\le1/4\), and the preceding size conditions. The column loss is distinct from the retained target fraction used in the next step.

2.9.8.7 Turn the trace inequality into coverage.

For the resulting family, use its actual total source rank \(R\). The two established tests now read

\[ \sum_i\|K_{ii}-I\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\le eR, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(Q^2)\le \frac{1+\eta}{D}(\operatorname{tr}Q)^2, \qquad 0\le e\le\tfrac14,\quad\eta\ge0. \]

The first gives \(T\ge R/2>0\). For the nonnegative eigenvalues \(q_1,\ldots,q_D\) of \(Q\), Cauchy–Schwarz then gives

\[ T^2\le\operatorname{rank}(Q)\sum_jq_j^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{rank}(Q)\ge\frac{D}{1+\eta}. \]

Since \(\ker Q=\bigcap_i\ker A_i^*\), the range of \(Q\) is precisely the span of the period images. Hence the images cover almost all target directions. The stronger form needed for slopes survives a further restriction \(S_i\subseteq V_i\): if \(\sum_i\operatorname{codim}S_i\le\alpha R\), then

\[ \dim\left(\sum_iA_iS_i\right)^\perp \le(\eta+4\alpha+4\sqrt e)D. \]

The next step applies this estimate to the high-slope pieces of the source lattices. The orbital calculation supplies the small errors; linear algebra then prevents all the arithmetically useful vectors from being confined to a small part of the common target.

The assembled correlation errors and their conversion to trace and frame bounds give the precise source statements.

2.9.9 Turn coverage into an arithmetic slope inequality.

For the integral sources, let \(\mu(N_i)\) be mean slope and \(\mu_{\max}(N_i)\) maximal slope, in the source’s common normalization. Subtract each map’s height allowance and define

\[ s=\frac{\sum_i r_i(\mu(N_i)-h_i)}{R}, \qquad U=\max_i\{\mu_{\max}(N_i)-h_i\}-s\ge0. \]

The slope filtration separates high-slope directions from low-slope ones. If a fraction \(\alpha\) of the adjusted source slopes is below \(s-\tau\), while none exceeds \(s+U\), their mean satisfies

\[ s\le\alpha(s-\tau)+(1-\alpha)(s+U) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha\le\frac{U}{U+\tau} \qquad(U>0,\ \tau\ge0). \]

Apply the coverage estimate to the remaining filtration subspaces. Integrality and the height bound ensure that a high-slope source image cannot acquire an arbitrarily low slope in the target: for a nonzero image, \(\mu_{\min}(B_iM)\ge\mu_{\min}(M)-h_i\). Integrating the resulting bounds over the slope thresholds controls the target’s mean slope.

Concretely, let \(W\) be the nonempty finite set of alternatives. The full target splits in the prescribed coordinates as \(H=H_C\oplus H_{\mathrm{bad}}\), where the lattice \(C\) spans \(H_C\), and the retained block of each \(\widetilde B_i\) is the matrix of \(B_i\) in the specified metrics. Suppose \(\dim H_C\ge(1-\delta)D\) with \(0\le\delta<1\), and choose \(H_*\ge\max\{0,s-\mu_{\min}(C)\}\). For these compatible integral maps and coordinates, the comparison gives

\[ s-\mu(C)\le\log|W|+ \frac{4U\log(1+H_*/U)+(\eta+4\sqrt e)H_*}{1-\delta}. \]

At \(U=0\), interpret \(U\log(1+H_*/U)\) by its limiting value zero. The logarithm comes from integrating \(U/(U+\tau)\). This is why a rate for the correlation error matters: it is multiplied by the potentially large scale \(H_*\). On the selected scales the construction makes that product small enough to enter the final error budget.

2.9.10 Cancel the large terms before estimating the error.

The slope comparison still contains terms much larger than a square-root error. Their cancellation uses the exact CM-period normalization. Let \(d>0\) be the absolute quadratic discriminant. Write \(\ell=\operatorname{Re}(L'(1,\chi_d)/L(1,\chi_d))\) for the selected quadratic character of discriminant \(-d\), let \(\gamma_E\) be Euler’s constant, and put \(v=\operatorname{rd}(E)/2>0\), half the host field’s root discriminant. The normalized period constant is

\[ \log\Omega=\gamma_E+\log(\pi/v)-\ell. \]

The CM-period theorem first supplies \(\gamma_E+\log(2\pi/\sqrt d)-\ell\); transferring to the host scale adds \(\log(\sqrt d/\operatorname{rd}(E))\). Keeping this scale change is essential.

Fix one translate \(u\), and abbreviate \(w_{u,j}(r)\) to \(w_{jr}\). Let \(j=1,\ldots,m\) index the lift stages and \(f\ge1\) be the number of positive weights \(w_{j1},\ldots,w_{jf}\). Independently, \(t\in T\) labels the selected top places, with degree parameters \(a_t>0\). Set \(A=\sum_{t\in T}a_t\), and abbreviate the counting error by \(E_{\mathrm{count}}\). The two large expressions are

\[ \begin{aligned} P&=m\sum_ta_t(\log a_t-1-\log\Omega),\\ Q_{\mathrm{count}}&= \sum_{j=1}^m\frac1f\sum_{r=1}^f\log(w_{jr}v/\pi) +mg_0-E_{\mathrm{count}}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(Q_{\mathrm{count}}\) is a lower bound for the logarithm of the relevant count, and \(g_0\) is the archimedean constant appearing in both comparisons. Expand the logarithms and substitute the period formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} P-AQ_{\mathrm{count}}+mAg_0 &=\mathcal M+A E_{\mathrm{count}},\\ \mathcal M&=\sum_{j,t}a_t\left( \log a_t-1-\gamma_E+\ell -\frac1f\sum_r\log w_{jr}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The \(g_0\) terms cancel directly. The \(mA\log(\pi/v)\) terms cancel because the period and count use the same host normalization. The surviving \(\mathcal M\) is the explicit main term used in the arithmetic prime-sum comparison. Finite Fourier inversion identifies the corresponding prime contribution; subtracting this main term leaves the corrected error that Landau can use. A bound on the uncancelled large expressions would lose the desired exponent.

2.10 From lattice geometry to a usable covolume bound.

The geometric branch controls the integral lattices underlying the automorphic construction. Its key mechanism is quantitative: removing a very small collection of bad directions costs little, provided their slopes cannot be too large. A lower bound for the remaining lattice’s slope becomes an upper bound for the logarithmic-covolume quantity used later.

2.10.1 Understand what a covolume measures.

As a model, take a full lattice \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n\), with basis vectors forming the columns of a matrix \(B\). Its covolume is the volume of one fundamental parallelepiped:

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)=|\det B| =\sqrt{\det(B^{\mathsf T}B)}. \]

The Gram matrix \(B^{\mathsf T}B\) records lengths and angles. Rescaling every vector by \(t>0\) multiplies the covolume by \(t^n\). Consequently the model slope \(\mu_{\mathrm{toy}}=-\log\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)/n\) changes by \(-\log t\). This explains the sign: a lower slope bound prevents the lattice’s fundamental volume from growing too much. The source uses arithmetic lattices over a number field, norms at its embeddings, and its own normalized squared-norm slopes; this Euclidean model motivates the calculation without replacing those definitions.

2.10.2 Put the two slope comparisons together.

Fix an eligible family, a sufficiently large scale \(x\), a compatible point, and a lift index \(i\). Write \(E_i\) for the positive error scale called unitE, \(T_i\) for mainT, and \(\mu_{\mathrm{core}},\mu_{\mathrm{type}},\mu_{\mathrm{good}}\) for the three source slopes. Two geometric inputs give, with fixed \(C_1,C_2\ge0\),

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{core}}\ge -T_i-C_2E_i, \qquad \mu_{\mathrm{type}}\ge\mu_{\mathrm{core}}-C_1E_i. \]

Substitution supplies the first useful bound:

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{type}}\ge -T_i-(C_1+C_2)E_i. \]

The first inequality comes from the deeper geometric construction. The second controls the loss when comparing that construction with the lattice at this lift stage. The main term \(T_i\) may have either sign; the positive scale \(E_i\) measures the allowed error. We still need to pass to the good part of the lattice.

2.10.3 Project onto good directions without losing integrality.

The projection \(P\) is idempotent and orthogonal at every relevant embedding:

\[ P^2=P,\qquad \|Pv+(w-Pw)\|_\sigma^2 =\|Pv\|_\sigma^2+\|w-Pw\|_\sigma^2. \]

Orthogonality controls the norms, but a rational projection need not preserve an integral lattice. The source therefore also supplies a positive integer \(N\) clearing its denominators:

\[ NP(\Lambda_{\mathrm{type}})\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathrm{type}}, \qquad 2\log N\le b\le E_i. \]

The denominator cost is thus paid for by the same error budget. This is why a vector-space decomposition alone would be insufficient.

The dependence on the bad rank can be seen directly. Let \(L=\Lambda_{\mathrm{type}}\ne0\), \(L_g=L\cap\operatorname{im}P\), and \(L_b=L\cap\ker P\), with ranks \(r_g,r_b\), so \(r_g+r_b>0\). The complementary projection sends \(L\) into \(N^{-1}L_b\): indeed \(N(1-P)v=Nv-NPv\in L_b\). Its kernel modulo \(L_b\) is exactly \(L_g+L_b\). Consequently the finite quotient \(L/(L_g+L_b)\) embeds in \(N^{-1}L_b/L_b\). The denominator cost is proportional to \(r_b\), not to the full rank.

Combining this index bound with orthogonality gives, in the source's squared-norm convention,

\[ (r_g+r_b)\mu(L) \le r_g\mu(L_g)+r_b\bigl(M+2\log N\bigr), \]

where \(M\) bounds the slope of the bad part. If \(r_b\le3\delta(r_g+r_b)\) and \(\delta\le1/12\), then \(r_g>0\) and

\[ \frac{r_b}{r_g}\le\frac{3\delta}{1-3\delta}\le4\delta. \]

Choose \(Q\ge0\) with \(M\le Q\), \(2\log N\le Q\), and \(-\mu(L)\le Q\). Rearranging the determinant inequality now explains the constant in the loss estimate:

\[ \mu(L_g)\ge\mu(L) -\frac{r_b}{r_g}\bigl(M+2\log N-\mu(L)\bigr) \ge\mu(L)-12\delta Q. \]

A small discarded proportion is useful precisely because both its slope and the denominator cost have controlled size.

2.10.4 Bound both the number and the size of bad directions.

Set \(\delta(x)=\exp(-x^{\eta/7})\), where \(\eta>0\) is fixed within the family. The source combines an exponentially small bad-rank proportion with a polynomial ceiling for the maximal slope:

\[ \operatorname{rk}\Lambda_{\mathrm{bad}} \le 3\delta(x)\operatorname{rk}\Lambda_{\mathrm{type}}, \qquad \mu_{\max}\le x^A b. \]

Its size conditions also give \(|T_i|\le x^B b\) and \(1\le b\le E_i\le x^B b\). These ingredients, together with the projection conditions and a nonzero good lattice, yield the precise slope-loss estimate

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{good}}\ge\mu_{\mathrm{type}}-\mathcal E_i(x), \qquad \mathcal E_i(x)= 12\delta(x)\bigl(x^A+x^B+C_1+C_2+1\bigr)E_i. \]

The small rank proportion controls how much can be discarded; the slope ceiling controls how expensive each discarded direction can be. Both are necessary.

2.10.5 Absorb the loss and reverse the slope inequality.

For fixed \(A,B,C_1,C_2,\eta\), exponential decay dominates every polynomial:

\[ 12e^{-x^{\eta/7}} \bigl(x^A+x^B+C_1+C_2+1\bigr)\longrightarrow0. \]

To see why, put \(c=\eta/7>0\). For any fixed exponent \(A\),

\[ e^{-x^c}x^A=\exp(A\log x-x^c)\longrightarrow0, \qquad \frac{\log x}{x^c}\longrightarrow0. \]

The negative term \( -x^c\) eventually dominates the logarithmic term. The same argument handles \(B\), and a fixed constant times \(e^{-x^c}\) also tends to zero. The conclusion concerns the error divided by \(E_i\), so it remains useful even when \(E_i\) itself grows.

Beyond a family-dependent threshold this factor is at most one, hence \(\mathcal E_i(x)\le E_i\). Combining the preceding steps gives

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{good}} \ge -T_i-(C_1+C_2+1)E_i. \]

Write \(\mathcal C_i\) for the source’s normalized logarithmic-covolume expression, namely its lcv quantity divided by the positive degree parameter \(D\). The recursive comparison states \(\mathcal C_i\le-\mu_{\mathrm{good}}\). Negating the last lower bound now gives the desired upper bound:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal C_i \le-\mu_{\mathrm{good}} \le T_i+(C_1+C_2+1)E_i.} \]

This is the numerical mechanism of the covolume estimate. The constituent inequalities specify the lattices, norms, and compatibility hypotheses. The loss calculation above follows the archive’s CovolumeFromGramAndBounds.lean, theorem lcv_le.

2.10.6 Follow the geometric lower bound from a deep cover back to the core.

The core-slope bound in 2.10.2 is obtained in two stages. First, heights and section counts control a lattice on a sufficiently deep level cover. Second, integral maps bring that control back to the original type. The estimates must be uniform as the field, weights, and level change. Here is the chain, including the losses that the large parameter \(b\) is chosen to absorb.

2.10.6.1 Choose a cover on which heights and section spaces can be compared.

At a nonterminal step, the cover is constructed by refining the level with the odd factor \(15^e\) and a suitable local ideal. The source's level construction uses its splitting and coprimality hypotheses; forgetting the added level structure gives the map back to the original moduli problem. It constructs the refined moduli scheme and enlarges the coefficient field to the compositum of the original field with a controlled ray-class field. This supplies the roots of unity and field-of-definition properties needed by the new level.

The depth is chosen as \(e=\lfloor3L_{\rm lev}/\log3\rfloor\), where \(L_{\rm lev}\) is the family's logarithmic level scale. In particular, \(e\log15\le9L_{\rm lev}\). The ray-class-field estimate bounds both field degree and root discriminant; after the family-size estimates it gives

\[ \log[F_Y:\mathbb Q] +\frac{\log|\operatorname{disc}F_Y|}{[F_Y:\mathbb Q]} \le391\zeta^2,\qquad \zeta=frL_{\rm lev}. \]

Thus deeper level comes with an explicit arithmetic cost. The family thresholds are then enlarged to satisfy the finitely many size, weight-gap, and norm inequalities together. The geometric existence theorem remains essential: parameter choices alone do not construct a moduli cover.

For this good deep cover \(Y\), the regularity results supply integral lattices of cusp sections at the required weights, finitely many nonempty geometric components, and a positive rank at the negatively shifted weight. These are substantive properties: a rational space of sections alone would not provide the integral lattice or the archimedean norms needed for an arithmetic degree.

On a component \(Z\), write \(h_Z\) for its normalized height, \(d_Z>0\) for its geometric degree, \(r_Z\) for the relevant section rank, and \(\widehat{\deg}_Z\) for its arithmetic degree. Let \(d\) be the flag dimension, \(T\) the truncation length, and

\[ B_T=\binom{T-1}{d},\qquad z=7\left(\frac{d^2}{T}+d\rho\right),\qquad 0\le\rho<1,\quad 0\le z<1. \]

The weight gaps give regularity and a birational section map; proportionality on the compactification compares its top degree with \(d_Z\). The comparison loses at most the factor \(1-\rho\). Degree and rank estimates then give, for one common \(d_*>0\),

\[ d_*\le d_Z\le\frac{d_*}{1-\rho},\qquad (1-z)r_Z\le B_Td_Z\le r_Z. \]

The binomial coefficient records successive hyperplane restrictions. The lower rank estimate also uses higher-cohomology vanishing so that sections lift through the restriction sequences. Thus these are estimates for actual section spaces, with controlled errors, rather than a formal monomial count. The cover and positivity hypotheses are what make this comparison available.

2.10.6.2 Convert short sections and volume information into component heights.

The short-section construction supplies integral generators on each component with a common norm bound \(\Lambda_{\rm sec}\ge1\). Set \(c_s=\log\Lambda_{\rm sec}\). The resulting projective height comparison gives

\[ h_Z+c_s\ge0. \]

This shift has a concrete purpose: an integral presentation by bounded sections makes the height nonnegative after paying for their norms. The source controls those norms using the level, coefficient field, flat degree, and weight size. One may take a bound of the form \(\Lambda_{\rm sec}=\exp(C(\zeta^3\|u_1\|_1+\zeta^5))\), where \(\zeta\) is the family's combined size parameter and \(u_1\) is the comparison weight; the parameter inequalities place the resulting cost inside the final error budget.

A separate volume estimate gives component comparison slopes \(s_Z\) with a lower bound on their ordinary mean. Multiplication by a controlled auxiliary section compares the relevant volumes; the birational map and top-degree comparison from the previous step turn this into

\[ (1-\rho)(s_Z+c_s)_+\le h_Z+c_s+c_c, \qquad \frac1M\sum_Zs_Z\ge-X, \qquad u_+=\max(u,0). \]

Here \(M\ge1\) is the number of components and \(c_c,X\ge0\). The comparison cost \(c_c\) includes the auxiliary-section multiplication cost and a metric-height correction. The source obtains the mean inequality from its mean-volume input; it does not deduce it merely from the existence of a deep cover. The positive part is necessary: a negative comparison slope gives no positive volume to exploit.

With \(E_i\ge1\) denoting the error scale already used in this section, the uniform estimates give a constant \(C_H\ge0\) such that

\[ TX\le C_HE_i,\qquad Tc_c\le C_HE_i,\qquad c_s\bigl(6d+10+T(z+2\rho)\bigr)\le C_HE_i. \]

These bounds explain why choosing short sections is not enough by itself. Their logarithmic norms must remain affordable after the truncation length and the degree-comparison losses have multiplied them.

2.10.6.3 Use Hilbert–Samuel at the chosen finite degree.

The arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel step estimates the determinant of the integral section lattice, not just its dimension. At the actual truncation length \(T\), its output is

\[ \widehat{\deg}_Z\ge B_Td_Z\left( Th_Z-3(d+1)|h_Z| -A_{\rm HS}\log(2+|h_Z|)-R_{\rm HS} \right), \]
\[ A_{\rm HS}>0,\quad R_{\rm HS}\ge0,\qquad A_{\rm HS}\log\!\bigl(\max(A_{\rm HS},1)\bigr)+R_{\rm HS} \le C_{\rm HS}E_i. \]

The term \(Th_Z\) is the height contribution that should dominate. The linear and logarithmic terms record the cost of turning that expectation into a bound for a finite lattice. In the numerical reduction, one can take

\[ A_{\rm HS}=26(26d^2+2zT),\qquad R_{\rm HS}=4000b+2c_{\rm twist}, \]

where \(c_{\rm twist}\ge0\) bounds the per-rank loss from the auxiliary twist. Write \(P\ge1\) for the family's auxiliary precision parameter and \(\kappa\ge0\) for the size of its central weight. The reduction uses \(d\ge1\), \(T\ge3d+5\), \(z\le\tfrac12\), and the family's degree-parameter bounds. In particular, it requires \(\log b\le P\), \(TP\le\sqrt b\), and \(zP\kappa\le b\), together with bounds for the degrees, norms, and denominators in the geometric Hilbert–Samuel input. These are checked before the component estimates are summed.

The explicit remainder matters. An asymptotic Hilbert–Samuel statement for a fixed variety would not control the changing varieties and weights in this family. The required result controls both the determinant degree and its errors at the selected value of \(T\).

2.10.6.4 Pass from the ordinary component mean to the lattice slope.

A bad component could dominate a rank-weighted sum even when the ordinary mean of the heights is acceptable. The degree and rank comparisons prevent this. Put \(R=\sum_Zr_Z>0\) and \(w_Z=B_Td_Z/R\). Then

\[ \sum_Zw_Z\le1,\qquad w_Z\ge\frac{(1-z)(1-\rho)}{M}. \]

Indeed, \(B_Td_Z\le r_Z\) gives the first statement. For the second, use \(d_Z\ge d_*\) and \(R\le MB_Td_*/((1-z)(1-\rho))\). Thus each component retains a definite share of the total weight.

Since \(h_Z+c_s+c_c\ge0\), multiply the height comparison by these weights, use their lower bound on the nonnegative positive-part terms, and use \(\sum_Zw_Z\le1\) for the common cost. This gives

\[ \sum_Zw_Z(h_Z+c_s) \ge(1-z)(1-\rho)^2(c_s-X)-c_c. \]

To see the error absorption explicitly, use the elementary bound

\[ A\log(2+x)\le x+A\log\max(A,1)+2 \qquad(A>0,\ x\ge0). \]

For \(A\ge1\), apply \(\log y\le y-1\) to \(y=(2+x)/A\); for \(A\le1\), use \(\log(2+x)\le x+2\). Put \(a=3d+4\), \(\beta=(1-z)(1-\rho)^2\), and let \(F(h)\) denote the bracket in the Hilbert–Samuel bound. Since \(|h|\le(h+c_s)+c_s\) when \(h\ge-c_s\),

\[ F(h)\ge(T-a)(h+c_s)-(T+a)c_s-C_{\rm HS}E_i-2. \]

Now \(T-a\ge1\). Multiply by \(w_Z\), sum, and insert the weighted-height bound. Using \(0\le\beta\le1\) gives

\[ \sum_Zw_ZF(h_Z)\ge -c_s\bigl(2a+T(1-\beta)\bigr) -T(X+c_c)-C_{\rm HS}E_i-2. \]

Finally, \(1-\beta\le z+2\rho\) and \(2a=6d+8\). The three height budgets bound the first term, \(TX\), and \(Tc_c\) separately by \(C_HE_i\); \(E_i\ge1\) absorbs the final constant. Combining this with the arithmetic-degree comparison gives the source's slightly relaxed uniform bound

\[ 2\,\frac{\sum_Z\widehat{\deg}_Z}{R} \le\mu_{\rm cover},\qquad \mu_{\rm cover}\ge-C_UE_i,\qquad C_U=2(3C_H+C_{\rm HS}+4). \]

This is the precise lower bound for the cover lattice at the untwisted comparison weight. The flat-line comparison subsequently restores the period-dependent main term:

\[ \mu_-\ge\mu_{\rm cover}-T_i-C_FE_i \ge-T_i-(C_F+C_U)E_i. \]

Here \(\mu_-\) is the slope at the negatively shifted weight and \(C_F\ge0\) controls the flat-line comparison. The component-to-slope theorem collects the hypotheses and states the constant \(C_U\).

2.10.6.5 Construct integral maps into the desired type.

A cover estimate still concerns the wrong lattice. Let \(\Gamma\) be the finite level group and \(\chi\) the character selecting the required type. Write \(\gamma^*\) for its pullback action. Choose a short integral cusp section \(u\) of auxiliary weight \(W\), so that the negative weight plus \(W\) is the desired central weight. Define the unnormalized twisted trace and the family of maps

\[ \operatorname{Tr}_{\chi}(v)= \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\chi(\gamma)^{-1}\gamma^*v, \qquad \phi_h(f)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\chi}(f\,h^*u) \quad(h\in\Gamma). \]

The action preserves the cusp lattice, \(u\) is integral, and finite-order character values are integral units. Consequently these maps carry integral sections into the desired character lattice \(B\). There is no division by \(|\Gamma|\). This preserves integrality while leaving an explicit norm cost.

The shortness and the determinant condition are obtained together. Orbit separation first supplies a cusp section \(u_0\) with a regular group determinant. Expand it in short integral generators supported on the components. When one coefficient varies, the determinant on an affected component is a polynomial of degree at most \(|\Gamma|\). A generator affects at most \(|\Gamma|\) components in its orbit. Hence at most \(|\Gamma|^2\) integer choices are forbidden, and one can replace that coefficient by an integer in \(\{0,\ldots,|\Gamma|^2\}\) while preserving regularity. Repeating this produces an integral \(u\) with bounded coefficients. The generator norm and pointwise bounds then control multiplication by \(u\).

In particular, the choice makes the group determinant

\[ \Delta(u)=\det\bigl((hg)^*u\bigr)_{h,g\in\Gamma} \]

a non-zero-divisor in the section ring: multiplication by it kills no nonzero section. The matrix identity behind the twisted traces therefore gives

\[ \bigl(\phi_h(f)=0\text{ for every }h\bigr)\Longrightarrow f=0. \]

Thus the whole family is injective, although an individual trace need not be. The non-zero-divisor condition is stronger than merely asking for one nonzero value of the determinant, because the section ring can have several components.

Suppose multiplication by any translate of \(u\) increases norm by at most \(B_u\), and the group action is isometric. Cauchy–Schwarz for each sum and then summation over \(h\) give

\[ \sum_{h\in\Gamma}\|\phi_h(f)\|_\sigma^2 \le|\Gamma|^3B_u^2\|f\|_\sigma^2 \le e^{2b}\|f\|_\sigma^2 \]

at every complex embedding \(\sigma\). In the construction, \(\log|\Gamma|\le45\zeta^2\) and \(B_u=\exp(C\zeta^9)\). The size choices ensure \(135\zeta^2+2C\zeta^9\le2b\). This accounts for the full operator-norm loss used by descent. The same determinant construction also gives \(n\,\operatorname{rk}B\le r_+\), where \(n=|\Gamma|\) and \(r_+\) is the section rank at the positively shifted weight. The integral-map theorem states these four outputs together: integrality, joint injectivity, the norm bound, and the rank comparison.

2.10.6.6 Budget the rank defect and return to the core.

Let \(A\) be the negative-weight lattice and let \(K_{\rm des}=P\kappa>0\) be the scalar descent budget. The rank-growth estimate and the preceding map construction imply

\[ n\,\operatorname{rk}B\le r_+ \le\left(1+\frac b{K_{\rm des}}\right)\operatorname{rk}A. \]

The excess target rank is therefore at most the fraction \(b/K_{\rm des}\). The source also constructs a spanning set of integral vectors in \(B\), each of norm at most \(e^{K_{\rm des}}\). This supplies control over the part of the target that the injective family does not fill. Put

\[ H=-T_i-(C_F+C_U)E_i-2b. \]

The lattice descent inequality, with the preceding norm and rank bounds, gives

\[ \mu_B\ge H-\frac b{K_{\rm des}}\bigl(|H|+2K_{\rm des}\bigr). \]

Finally, comparison with the original coefficient field charges twice its logarithmic root discriminant. The good-cover field-size bound places that charge inside \(2b\), giving

\[ \mu_{\rm core}\ge\mu_B- 2\frac{\log|\operatorname{disc}F_Y|}{[F_Y:\mathbb Q]} \ge\mu_B-2b. \]

Use \(b\le E_i\), \(b\le K_{\rm des}\), and \(|T_i|\le K_{\rm des}\). Then \(b/K_{\rm des}\le1\), the product \((b/K_{\rm des})|T_i|\) is at most \(b\), and \((b/K_{\rm des})2K_{\rm des}=2b\). Expanding the absolute value by the triangle inequality yields the explicit conclusion

\[ \boxed{\mu_{\rm core}\ge -T_i-\bigl(2(C_F+C_U)+9\bigr)E_i.} \]

This is the starting inequality needed in 2.10.2. The terminal step uses a separate low-defect bound; the family takes the larger of its constant and the constant displayed here. The chain explains how heights become an integral core-lattice bound. Its geometric inputs still carry precise hypotheses about models, positivity, volume estimates, and finite-degree Hilbert–Samuel; the original geometric assembly records where those are supplied.

2.10.7 Make the estimate uniform and carry it to the same data.

The constants and thresholds are chosen for an eligible family, before the later point and lift index. With \(C_0=C_1+C_2+1\), the result has the order of quantifiers

\[ \exists C_0\ge0,\ x_0\quad \forall x\ge x_0,\ \forall\text{ eligible }p,\ \forall i, \forall C\ge C_0:\quad \mathcal C_i(p)\le T_i(p)+CE_i(p). \]

Eligibility includes the family’s base threshold and the required correspondence ties. Taking the maximum of finitely many thresholds makes the component bounds hold together. The transport theorem then carries this estimate to the compatible selections used by the theta and Galois branches. It becomes one geometric input to the prime-sum comparison; the cohomology bound supplies another. The ensuing interval estimate is what the Landau argument can use.

2.10.8 Keep a local derivative from vanishing.

A local divisibility estimate must be accompanied by a nonzero object to which it applies. Normalize the additive valuation by \(v_p(p)=1\), with \(v_p(0)=+\infty\). A lower bound \(v_p(J)\ge b\) says that \(J\) is divisible to at least the prescribed order; it allows \(J=0\). An upper bound \(v_p(J)\le B<\infty\) instead guarantees that the chosen derivative survives cancellation. Both directions enter the local construction.

A jet is a weighted derivative at the origin. If \(P=\sum_\nu P_\nu X^\nu\) is a polynomial and \(F\) a formal power series, then

\[ P(\partial)F(0)=\sum_\nu P_\nu\,\nu!\,[X^\nu]F. \]

The factorials are essential arithmetic factors. In one variable on each side, take an odd \(i\ge1\), a prime \(p\ge5\), and an integer \(y\ge0\). Set

\[ e_i=1+\frac{p^i-p}{p^2-1},\qquad F(X,Y)=\exp(-p^{-e_i}X^{p^i}Y),\qquad J_y=\partial_X^{p^iy}\partial_Y^yF(0,0). \]

Only the term with exponential index \(y\) can contribute. Differentiation in \(Y\) cancels its \(y!\) denominator, while differentiation in \(X\) leaves

\[ J_y=(-p^{-e_i})^y(p^iy)!,\qquad v_p(J_y)=y\left(\frac{p(p^i+1)}{p^2-1}-1\right) -\frac{s_p(y)}{p-1}, \]

Here \(s_p(y)\) is the sum of the base-\(p\) digits of \(y\). The identity follows from \(v_p(n!)=(n-s_p(n))/(p-1)\) and \(s_p(p^iy)=s_p(y)\). It exhibits a nonzero derivative and its exact valuation.

For \(r\times r\) matrices of variables with \(r\ge1\), replace the exponent by \(-p^{-e_i}\sum_{a,b}X_{ab}^{p^i}Y_{ab}+h_1(X,Y)\) and take the jet with \(\det(\partial_X)^{p^iy}\det(\partial_Y)^y\). Different determinant terms can now cancel. The source separates an explicit factorial factor from a binomial sum:

\[ J_y=(-p^{-e_i})^{ry}C_r(p^iy)B_y, \quad C_r(k)=\prod_{a=0}^{r-1}\frac{(k+a)!}{a!}, \quad B_y=\sum_{s=0}^y\binom ys a_s,\quad a_0=1. \]

The perturbation is constrained. Every monomial of \(h_1\) must contain a \(Y\)-variable. For a coefficient \(c\) of total \(X\)-degree \(u\) and total \(Y\)-degree \(v\), require

\[ \begin{aligned} v_p(c)&\ge\frac1{p-1} -u\left(\rho_p+\frac1{2p^i}-\delta_p\right) -v\left(\rho_p+\frac12-\delta_p\right),\\ \rho_p&=\frac1{p^2-1},\qquad\delta_p=\frac1{8p^i}. \end{aligned} \]

These bounds leave enough valuation gain to dominate the factorial losses. Under these hypotheses the source proves \(v_p(a_s)>0\) once

\[ s\ge S=\left\lceil64r^2\bigl(i+3+3\log_p(r+1)\bigr)\right\rceil. \]

For each integer \(Y_0\ge0\), there must be an integer \(y\in[Y_0,Y_0+S)\) with \(v_p(B_y)\le0\). Otherwise remove the positive-valuation tail and consider the remaining \(S\) binomial combinations. Their coefficient matrix is \(M_{j,s}=\binom{Y_0+j}{s}\), for \(0\le j,s<S\). Successive forward differences turn it into an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries one. Hence \(\det M=1\), and its inverse has integer entries. If every combination had positive valuation, every \(a_s\) with \(s<S\) would too, contradicting \(a_0=1\).

The conclusion is a good derivative somewhere in a controlled short interval, rather than at every prescribed order. That flexibility lets the later local construction round degree allocations to admissible integer orders. The geometric identification with actual theta sections and the coefficient bounds remain essential inputs to this calculation.

The final assembly

2.11 Choose the parameters, then exclude a zero.

The final assembly is more than a list of inputs. The constructions must refer to the same data, their constants must be chosen in the right order, and their estimates must hold throughout the intervals used by the analytic argument. Here is that logic with the intermediate inequalities made explicit.

2.11.1 Obtain one family satisfying all the required conditions.

The theta, geometric, and Galois constructions supply a compatible family. Schematically, the assertion is

\[ \exists D,\quad \mathcal A(D)\land\mathcal F(D)\land\mathcal G(D), \]

where the three predicates stand for the required arithmetic, functional, and geometric conditions. This single witness matters: an arithmetic bound for one family and a geometric bound for an unrelated family cannot be substituted into the same comparison. The source’s constructor also includes the definite-side condition needed downstream. It constructs these conditions without assuming Dirichlet nonvanishing.

The selection has a precise order. Let \(K\) encode lower requirements on auxiliary parameters, \(P(F,K)\) mean that a family \(F\) meets them, \(B(F)\) collect the comparison bounds, and \(W(F)\) be the required arithmetic-window property. The useful combination is

\[ \begin{gathered} \bigl[\exists K\;\forall F:\ P(F,K)\Rightarrow B(F)\bigr]\ \land\\ \bigl[\forall K\;\exists F:\ P(F,K)\land W(F)\bigr]\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\exists F:\ B(F)\land W(F). \end{gathered} \]

First obtain sufficient requirements for every comparison, combine them by taking their componentwise maxima, and intersect the allowed ranges of \(\varepsilon\). Then invoke existence with those combined requirements to select one family carrying the window. Every comparison applies to that family. Separate assertions \(\exists F\,B(F)\) and \(\exists F\,W(F)\) would not justify the conclusion. This selection happens before the later maximum of scale thresholds in step 2.11.4.

2.11.2 Fix the loss exponents before choosing a small parameter.

Fix the analytic character family \(\mathcal F\), so its prime-sum error \(G_{\mathcal F}\) is fixed. The compatible arithmetic and geometric data, together with the Green representation input, supply the aggregate comparison. The arithmetic window provides a realization for that character family. After these steps, the useful numerical conclusion has the form

\[ G_{\mathcal F}(x)\le x^{1/2+A\varepsilon}, \qquad A=e_D+e_L+7, \]

on the permitted windows, beyond a threshold. Crucially, the exponents \(e_D,e_L\) and a positive admissibility limit \(\varepsilon_*\) are fixed before \(\varepsilon\) is chosen. Suitable auxiliary realizations are available for every \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_*\). The realization and the eventual scale may depend on \(\varepsilon\); the analytic character family \(\mathcal F\) remains fixed. If \(A\) were allowed to grow like \(1/\varepsilon\), shrinking \(\varepsilon\) would not necessarily bring the exponent toward one half.

The earlier arithmetic comparison explains the numerical bound. The deeper Green representation theorem remains an input at this stage; the parameter calculation below does not replace its proof.

2.11.3 Place the error exponent strictly before a candidate zero.

Suppose a candidate zero has real part \(\sigma>1/2\), away from the pole exception. Define a positive margin and an intermediate exponent by

\[ m=\min\{\sigma-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\}, \qquad \theta=\tfrac12+\frac m2. \]

Because \(0<m\le1/2\) and \(m\le\sigma-1/2\), we have

\[ \tfrac12<\theta\le\tfrac34<1, \qquad \theta\le\tfrac12+\frac{\sigma-1/2}{2}<\sigma. \]

Now choose, for example,

\[ \varepsilon= \min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_*}{2}, \frac{m}{2(|A|+1)}\right\}. \]

Both entries are positive, so \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_*\). Moreover,

\[ A\varepsilon\le |A|\varepsilon \le\frac{|A|}{|A|+1}\frac m2 \le\frac m2, \qquad \tfrac12+A\varepsilon\le\theta<\sigma. \]

The absolute value makes the choice valid for any fixed real \(A\). This is the explicit reason “arbitrarily close to one half” is enough to rule out every fixed point strictly to its right. No uniform positive distance from the critical line is assumed.

2.11.4 Choose a late window after all thresholds are known.

Let \(X_*\ge1\) dominate the finitely many thresholds needed for the comparison and this choice of \(\varepsilon\). The window theorem supplies arbitrarily late intervals; with the final assembly’s parameter \(3/2\), choose

\[ Z\ge X_*, \qquad x\in[Z,Z^{3/2}]. \]

Every point in that interval satisfies \(x\ge1\). Increasing an exponent therefore increases the corresponding power, and step 2.11.3 gives

\[ G_{\mathcal F}(x) \le x^{1/2+A\varepsilon} \le x^\theta \qquad(Z\le x\le Z^{3/2}). \]

This is an upper bound, not an absolute-value estimate. The Landau theorem used here is designed for precisely that one-sided information. A maximum of thresholds is legitimate because the estimates hold at all later eligible scales; the existence of arbitrarily late windows then places a whole interval beyond that maximum.

2.11.5 Apply the analytic criterion at the chosen exponent.

The Landau argument excludes zeros with real part greater than \(\theta\) from the selected character family. The hypothetical zero satisfies \(\sigma>\theta\), so it is excluded. Since the candidate point was arbitrary, the primitive-character conclusion holds throughout \(\operatorname{Re}s>1/2\), apart from the pole exception.

There is no need to take a limiting theorem at \(\theta=1/2\). For each proposed zero, a single choice of \(\theta\) strictly between one half and its real part is sufficient.

2.11.6 Pass to all characters, then reflect.

If \(\chi\) modulo \(q\) is induced from a primitive character \(\chi^*\) of conductor \(q^*\), then at the permitted points \(\chi\ne\mathbf1\) or \(s\ne1\), the finite Euler-factor identity is

\[ L(s,\chi)=L(s,\chi^*) \prod_{\substack{p\mid q\\p\nmid q^*}} \bigl(1-\chi^*(p)p^{-s}\bigr). \]

For \(\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>0\), each extra factor is nonzero, because

\[ |\chi^*(p)p^{-s}|\le p^{-\sigma}<1. \]

Thus the primitive nonvanishing result extends to all Dirichlet characters at the permitted points. The functional equation then sends a left-hand zero in the critical strip to a forbidden right-hand zero for the inverse primitive character. The next argument checks the gamma factors and endpoint conditions needed for that last deduction.

The final deduction

3 From nonvanishing to the critical line.

Having located the theorem that supplies the input, we now explain the short conditional deduction in full. The argument below can also be read independently by assuming H as stated above.

The theorem

3.1 A zero in the strip must lie on the line.

Conditional critical-line theorem. Assume \(H\). Let \(q\ge1\), let \(\chi\) be primitive modulo \(q\), and let \(s\in\mathbb C\) satisfy \(0<\operatorname{Re}s<1\). If \(L(s,\chi)=0\), then \(\operatorname{Re}s=\tfrac12\).

This is the mathematical content of Formalism.VW.critLine. It uses the classical functional equation and gamma-factor properties just described. Its two cases have a simple purpose: a zero on the right contradicts \(H\) immediately; a zero on the left is moved to the right before applying \(H\).

The strip assumptions also give \(s\ne0\) and \(s\ne1\). After reflection, they give \(1-s\ne0\) and \(1-s\ne1\). Thus every evaluation used below avoids the exceptional endpoints.

The argument, step by step

3.2 Exclude both sides of the critical line.

3.2.1 Suppose the zero is off the line.

Write \(\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s\), and assume for a contradiction that \(\sigma\ne\tfrac12\). The order on the real numbers gives exactly two possibilities: \(\sigma>\tfrac12\) or \(\sigma<\tfrac12\).

3.2.2 On the right, apply the input directly.

If \(\sigma>\tfrac12\), apply \(H\) with \(\psi=\chi\) and \(w=s\). The half-plane condition holds by this case assumption. The pole exception is satisfied because \(s\ne1\), even if \(\chi\) is principal. We obtain \(L(s,\chi)\ne0\), contradicting the hypothesized zero.

3.2.3 On the left, first complete the zero.

Now suppose \(\sigma<\tfrac12\). Because \(0<\sigma\), the completion factor at \(s\) is finite and nonzero. Multiplying the zero \(L(s,\chi)=0\) by that factor yields

\[\Lambda(s,\chi)=0.\]

There is no limiting or numerical step here. The completion is an ordinary product of well-defined complex values at this point. The positivity of the real part has ruled out gamma poles.

3.2.4 Reflect the zero and invert the character.

The character \(\overline\chi=\chi^{-1}\) is primitive and has the same parity and conductor. Apply its functional equation at \(1-s\). Since taking the conjugate twice returns \(\chi\), it gives

\[\Lambda(1-s,\overline\chi) =\varepsilon(\overline\chi)\Lambda(s,\chi)=0.\]

In this direction, only multiplication by the functional-equation factor is needed. The source makes the same choice by applying the functional equation for the inverse character.

3.2.5 Remove the completion on the right.

Put \(w=1-s\). Its real part satisfies

\[\operatorname{Re}w=1-\sigma>\tfrac12, \qquad 0<\operatorname{Re}w<1.\]

The completion factor for \(\overline\chi\) at \(w\) is again finite and nonzero. Dividing the completed zero by it therefore gives \(L(w,\overline\chi)=0\).

But \(H\) applies to exactly this character and this point. We have \(\operatorname{Re}w>\tfrac12\), and \(w\ne1\) because \(s\ne0\). It follows that \(L(w,\overline\chi)\ne0\), the required contradiction.

3.2.6 Both alternatives are impossible.

A zero in the open strip can be neither to the right nor to the left of the critical line. Hence \(\operatorname{Re}s=\tfrac12\). This completes the conditional proof.

3.2.7 Recover RH with the trivial zeros accounted for.

The character of modulus one gives zeta, so \(H\) supplies \(\zeta(s)\ne0\) for \(\operatorname{Re}s>1/2\), apart from \(s=1\). The usual RH statement also explicitly sets aside the negative even integers. To see the complete deduction, suppose \(\zeta(\rho)=0\), \(\rho\ne1\), and \(\rho\notin\{-2,-4,-6,\ldots\}\).

A point to the right of the line is already excluded. If \(\operatorname{Re}\rho<1/2\), then \(\operatorname{Re}(1-\rho)>1/2\). Also \(\rho\ne0\), because \(\zeta(0)=-1/2\). Apply the zeta functional equation:

\[ \zeta(\rho)=2^\rho\pi^{\rho-1} \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi\rho}{2}\right) \Gamma(1-\rho)\zeta(1-\rho). \]

The exponential factors never vanish. The gamma factor is finite and nonzero since its argument has positive real part. The reflected zeta factor is nonzero by \(H\), and its argument is not \(1\). Finally, the sine factor vanishes only when \(\rho\) is an even integer. In this left half-plane those are zero and the negative even integers, all excluded above. Every factor is therefore nonzero, a contradiction. Only \(\operatorname{Re}\rho=1/2\) remains. The zeta deduction in the original source checks these same exceptions.
